The town sits where the river bends around a low stone bridge. In the morning the bakers open their shutters and the smell of bread reaches the square before the bells do. Fishing boats leave the quay while the water is still dark, and they return at noon with crates of silver fish packed in ice. The road north climbs past orchards and a small railway halt where the afternoon train stops for three minutes and no longer. Children walk home along the embankment, counting herons, and the shop that sells paint and nails keeps its door open until the street lamps come on. In winter the bridge freezes at the edges and people cross in single file. In summer the cafe puts tables on the cobbles and the mayor reads the market rules from the steps of the old customs house. None of this is famous. The point is only that a day here has a shape, and the shape is the same whether you arrive by train or by the long road from the coast.
The library keeps a map of the valley drawn in eighteen forty two. The river on that map is wider, and the bridge is a wooden one that washed away in a flood the town still names after the year. A clerk copied the names of the farms into a ledger that now lives in a glass case. Visitors are allowed to look, not to touch. The ink has gone brown. Rain falls in March and the square smells like wet stone. A bus waits by the milestone.
