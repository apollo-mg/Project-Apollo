# Dev Diary: The Resonance War (2026-01-28)

Today was a brutal dance between mechanical precision and the stubborn limits of my local compute. I set out to solve the "phantom vibration" plague on the CoreXY frame, and while we've made strides, the friction between the "Soul" (the elegance of a perfectly balanced motion system) and the "System" (the sheer noise of the hardware) remains high.

### The Ghost in the Machine
The day began with a pursuit of pure signal. I removed the fan from the toolhead to isolate the noise floor. The result? A cleaner graph, but a persistent, high-frequency spike at 150Hz on Belt A. 

The mystery deepened: the vibration wasn't "audible" as a rattle when tested with the `EXCITATE` tool. This is the kind of technical ambiguity that kills momentum. Is it electronic interference? A harmonic from the belt teeth? Or a subtle, silent rub of the belt against a flange? The distinction matters—one is a software filter problem, the other is a physical manufacturing flaw.

### The Tension Tug-of-War
I spent significant time on belt tensioning. After adjusting, the delta between Belt A and B jumped from 3.2 Hz to 7.2 Hz. It was a moment of architectural frustration—the system was fighting itself. But, after a heavy-handed adjustment of Belt A, we achieved a perfect match. The graphs aligned beautifully at lower frequencies. The mechanics are now balanced, but the high-frequency ghost is still haunting the X-axis.

### The Token Wall
Working with Gemini in this local-first setup is a double-edged sword. The intelligence is immense, but we hit a massive wall with the VRAM-to-Token bottleneck. Every time we tried to analyze the high-res `.png` resonance graphs, the binary data exploded the context window, crashing the session. 

I had to rethink the architecture of our interaction. We couldn't just "read" the images; we had to engineer a workaround. I used `ffmpeg` to downsample the graphs on the Raspberry Pi, shrinking them into manageable-sized thumbnails. This allowed us to bypass the token limit, providing enough resolution for the AI to "see" the frequency peaks without choking the context window. It was a victory of engineering over raw compute.

### The Final Shaper Verdict
We finally ran the `AXES_SHAPER_CALIBRATION`. The results were sobering:

* **X-Axis:** The system is incredibly stiff. It's demanding a `2HUMP_EI` shaper at 124.4 Hz. The potential acceleration is massive (~16,400 mm/s²).
* **Y-Axis:** This is where the "Soul" meets the hard reality of the frame. The Y-axis is struggling with a resonance at ~54 Hz, capping our acceleration at a mere ~8,750 mm/s². 

The discrepancy between X and Y is clear now. The Y-axis is carrying a structural resonance that the X-axis doesn't have. We've tuned the software to account for the vibration, but the physical frame is still "breathing" in a way that limits our performance.

### Lessons for the Multi-Agent Ecosystem
1. **Context Management is as vital as Quantization:** In a local-first AI world, the ability to move data (like resizing images on the edge) is more important than having a massive model. We can't just throw more VRAM at the problem; we have to be smarter about how we feed the model.
2. **Mechanical-Digital Feedback Loops:** The AI is no longer just a "chatbot"; it is a diagnostic tool. It's interpreting the physics of a motor-driven gantry. The future of local AI isn't in text; it's in the intersection of signal processing and machine learning.

**Next Steps:** Apply the `FIRMWARE_RESTART`. See if the 150Hz ghost disappears under real-world printing loads, or if we're looking at a hardware redesign for the Y-axis.
