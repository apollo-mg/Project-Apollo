# Apollo Dev Diary - 2026-03-06

### The Crash and the Lost Flag

The session started with a crash. The terminal died, taking its history with it. We were left staring at a performance-degraded machine, unable to recall the exact command that unlocked our GPU. The silence of the `~/.bash_history` file was deafening. 

We had to reverse-engineer our own brilliance. The clues were in the logs: `low perfcap`. The only logical path back to full performance was the hardware override we had used previously. We found it buried in memory: `--setperflevel high`. 

It was a stark reminder of the fragility of our setup. We aren't running on a managed cloud; we're walking a tightrope on consumer silicon. Getting that flag back into the systemd override was the first victory, but the real war was just beginning.

### The RDNA 4 Reality Check

I tried to spin up our massive Qwen 3.5 models via Docker, and the system slammed into a wall. The error was explicit:

```
RuntimeError: Get GPU arch from rocminfo failed "Unknown GPU architecture: gfx1201. Supported architectures: [...]"
```

This is the **AMD Tax**. While NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem is a paved highway, ROCm on consumer hardware is a dirt track. The standard `rocm/vllm:latest` image is compiled for Instinct datacenter cards (MI300X), and it completely lacks the architecture whitelist for our **RX 9070 XT (gfx1201)**. 

We were trying to force a square peg into a round hole. The `latest` tag is 3 months old, compiled for enterprise GPUs, and simply doesn't know how to speak to RDNA 4.

### The "Navi" Dev Image Epiphany

The breakthrough came from a forgotten file: `run_vllm_benchmark.sh`. We had previously saved a reference to a bleeding-edge developer image:
`rocm/vllm-dev:rocm7.2_navi_ubuntu24.04_py3.12_pytorch_2.9_vllm_0.14.0rc0`

This isn't a standard release. It's an internal engineering build from AMD, specifically patched to support consumer Navi cards. It contains the Triton and AITer tuning that bridges the gap between the software and our silicon. 

We pulled this image. It's heavy (~30GB), but it's the only bridge we have. We locked this discovery into our global memory banks. No future agent running on Apollo will make the mistake of pulling the enterprise `latest` tag for this hardware.

### Bare Metal vs. The Container War

We tried to run PyTorch directly on the bare metal, applying 14 surgical patches to the source code to fix `Wave32` and `Normalization.cuh` templates. It compiled, but the kernel execution resulted in an **illegal memory access**. 

The conclusion is inescapable: **Bare-metal PyTorch is not stable on consumer RDNA 4 without an entire team of AMD engineers maintaining the fork.** 

We had to retreat to the Docker environment. The Navi Dev image is the only stable ground we have. It's a "Sovereign Engine" reality: we must rely on the curated, albeit bleeding-edge, builds that AMD's internal teams provide for consumer hardware.

### FP8: The Golden Path

With the Docker engine finally alive, we faced a choice for our **Gatekeeper** model (Qwen 3.5 4B): GGUF or FP8?

For RDNA 4, **FP8 is the Golden Path.** 
1.  **Hardware Acceleration:** RDNA 4 has dedicated instructions for 8-bit float. We get a 2x theoretical throughput increase over FP16.
2.  **VRAM Efficiency:** FP8 cuts our memory footprint in half compared to FP16, leaving more room for the KV cache.
3.  **Precision:** Unlike INT8, FP8 maintains the dynamic range needed for high-IQ reasoning without the hallucination risks of integer quantization.

We swapped the GGUF for an FP8 Safetensor. We configured the vLLM container to use a **`gpu-memory-utilization` of 0.20**, locking the Gatekeeper into roughly 3.2GB of VRAM. This leaves 12.8GB for Ollama to hot-swap our Engineer and Specialist models. It's the perfect "Sweet Spot" for a 16GB card.

### VRAM Tetris and the Architecture

The final architecture is now crystallized:

*   **vLLM (Docker):** Resident on Port 8000. Runs the **Qwen 3.5 4B Aggressive (FP8)**. Instant-on triage, 100+ TPS, no system prompt re-processing.
*   **Ollama (Host):** Resident on Port 11434. Runs the **9B Engineer** and **14B Specialist**. Uses dynamic memory swapping for the heavier models.

We learned the hard way that a 14B model (15GB weights) cannot coexist with a 0.90 utilization setting on a 16GB card with a desktop environment. That's 30GB of demand on 16GB of hardware. We had to dial it back to 0.80 to make it work.
