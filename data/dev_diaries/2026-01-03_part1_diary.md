# Dev Diary: 2026-01-03 | Tuning the Beast

The lights are low, the RX 9070 XT is humming in the background as it churns through local model training, and the terminal is my only window into the machine. Today wasn't about writing new code; it was about reconciling the math with the physical reality of high-speed motion. It was about teaching the machine to stop fighting itself.

## The Ghost in the Resonance

We started deep in the weeds of input shaping. I was looking at the ringing tower results, and the data was... weird. The automated calibration script was screaming about a 150Hz peak—the carbon beam is stiff, clearly, but the actual visual artifact, the "ghost" on the print surface, was manifesting at a lower, much more obnoxious 70-75Hz.

This is the classic friction between the **System** and the **Soul**. The algorithm sees the 150Hz spike and wants to optimize for it to maximize theoretical acceleration. But the human eye? The eye sees the wide, low-frequency ripples. Those 75Hz waves are what ruin a print. 

We made a call to ignore the "correct" mathematical peak and instead targeted the visual resonance with the MZV shaper. It feels like cheating the physics, but by silencing the 75Hz trough, we’ve essentially hidden the high-frequency noise in the texture of the filament. It’s a hack, but a beautiful one.

## High-Flow Physics: Beyond the 0.5mm Limit

Once the motion system was stabilized, we moved to the fluid dynamics. I’m running a Goliath hotend—it’s a monster built for high-flow, but my previous profiles were treating it like a standard nozzle. 

We spent an hour dissecting the line-width logic. If you’re using a 0.5mm nozzle on a high-flow setup, you don't print like a hobbyist; you print like a machinist. We shifted the logic toward "Goliath Tuning." We’re talking 0.6mm to 0.8mm widths for infill. We’re pushing the volumetric flow to the limit, using the width to create structural integrity that the motion system can actually support. It’s about leveraging the "squish" of the plastic to fight the sheer speed of the CoreXY movement.

## The Profile Audit: From Sedan to F1

The most productive part of the session was the JSON audit. I fed the current profiles into Gemini, and it tore them apart. 

It turns out I was driving a Formula 1 car like a family sedan. 

The profiles were choked. The acceleration limits in the slicer were set to 20,000 mm/s², but the Klipper firmware was capping them at 16,000. The Z-hop was non-existent, which is a death sentence for high-speed travel—one tiny curl on an overhang and the nozzle becomes a wrecking ball. And the retraction? 0.8mm was pulling molten plastic back into the cold zone, risking the kind of heat creep that kills a long print.

We rebuilt the `Ender 6 0.5mm` profile from the ground for high-speed reality:
*   **Lowered retraction** to 0.5mm to protect the LGX Lite.
*   **Enabled Wipe-on-loop** to handle the oozing of the high-flow melt zone.
*   **Synced acceleration** to the 16k firmware ceiling.
*   **Shifted the Infill Pattern** from Cubic to Cross Hatch. Cubic is fine for slow stuff, but at 500mm/s, you need a pattern that doesn't cause the nozzle to self-destruct by crossing its own path.

## The Takeaway

Today was a reminder that local AI development isn't just about weights and biases; it's about the interface between digital intent and physical execution. Whether I'm tuning a quantization strategy for a local LLM or a resonance frequency for a Klipper firmware, the goal is the same: **minimizing the error between the command and the result.**

The machine is finally starting to move with intention. Now, we see if it can handle the speed.
