# Dev Diary: 2026-03-02 (Part 15)

## The Corporate Spy in the Driveway

I spent the morning descending into the rabbit hole of LexisNexis, and frankly, it’s making me more certain of the Project Apollo mission than I’ve ever been. I’m looking at the architectural implications of a world where "privacy" is just a marketing term used to mask a massive, automated, persistent surveillance engine.

I've been digging into Jason LaRue. He’s an AVP at LexisNexis, based right here in Martinsville. On the surface, he's a tech veteran, a pillar of the community. But the deeper I look, the more the "Soul" of his work clashes with the "System." He’s at the helm of Coplogic—a division that handles massive data pipelines for law enforcement.

The real gut-punch came when I looked into the 2024 GM/OnStar scandal. It turns out that for years, General Motors has been secretly harvesting 258+ data points per trip—braking, speed, acceleration—and selling it straight to LexisNexis. They weren't just "helping you drive better," as the marketing suggested. They were building a permanent, un-erasable digital record of your every move to sell to insurance companies. 

It’s a massive, automated, persistent surveillance engine. If you're a citizen, you don't just have a car; you have a spy in your driveway.

This is exactly the kind of "Corporate State" infrastructure I’m trying to escape with Project Apollo. This is why we need local, air-gapped, sovereign intelligence. When the "Cloud" is just a way to turn your behavior into a subscription model for an insurance company, the only way to win is to own your own silicon.

I'm meeting him for coffee soon. It’s a weird psychological tension. I’m a truth-seeker, a "Red Team" architect. He's a high-level corporate manager who likely oversees the very data pipelines that enable this surveillance. I don't know if he's the one pulling the trigger, or just the guy responsible for the budget that allows the trigger to be pulled. 

But that's the point. In a world of massive, centralized, cloud-dependent systems, the individual is a data-point. In the Apollo ecosystem, the individual is the architect. 

I'm going into this meeting not to find a job, but to perform reconnaissance. I want to see if there's a human under that corporate title—a man who misses the era of "building things that work" before they were turned into "data products." If he's part of the machine, I'll know exactly what kind of monster I'm building my countermeasures against.

If he's the guy who sees the "Holy Shit" reality of this data-brokerage every day, then we're not enemies. We're just on different sides of the same fight.
