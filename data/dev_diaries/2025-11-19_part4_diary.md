# Project Apollo: Dev Diary
## November 19, 2025 - The Silicon Rebellion

### The AMD Gambit

I'm staring at the console output, watching the green text scroll across my black terminal window, and I can't help but feel a certain kinship with the silicon beneath my fingertips. That RX 9070 XT doesn't want to play nice. It never has. It's been designed for rasterization, for texture mapping, for the predictable chaos of gaming workloads—not for the wild unpredictability of transformer attention mechanisms.

Yet here we are, forcing square pegs into round holes with ZLUDA as our crowbar.

The system started up with deceptive elegance. "Starting XTTS-WebUI (ZLUDA Mode)" flashed across the screen, a beacon of false hope. For a moment, I believed we might actually get away with this. The model loaded—0.66 seconds to device, the log proudly announced. Latents pre-created for the three speakers. Everything seemed to be working.

Then came the crash.

### The Schema Betrayal

```
TypeError: argument of type 'bool' is not iterable
```

That's the sound of a dream dying. Or at least, that's how it felt when Gradio's schema processor choked on its own complexity. The irony wasn't lost on me: our system for generating human speech was failing because it couldn't properly parse a boolean flag.

The error was buried deep in `gradio_client/utils.py`, in a function called `_json_schema_to_python_type`. It was expecting a dictionary, got a boolean, and decided that was grounds for a complete system shutdown. How pedestrian. How utterly human.

I patched it. Of course I patched it. That's what we do in this line of work. We don't accept limitations handed to us by maintainers who've never had to squeeze a transformer model onto consumer hardware against their will.

### The First Breath

After the patch, the system finally breathed. "Running on local URL: http://127.0.0.1:8010" glowed in the terminal like a Christmas tree in a bunker.

Then came the real test. Voice cloning. The user uploaded a sample, typed some text, and clicked "Generate."

And waited.

And waited.

200 seconds. 300 seconds. The console silent as a crypt.

This is where the philosophical meets the physical. Where our grand ambitions for synthetic consciousness collide with the brutal reality of JIT compilation on emulated CUDA cores.

```
creating latents for F:\AI\ComfyUI\ComfyUI-Zluda\xtts-webui\temp\resampled_audio_1db1790f-0ffb-4662-a189-5169f2dced3b.wav
```

That's all the feedback we got. No progress bars. No percentage complete. Just a silent calculation happening somewhere in the liminal space between CPU and GPU, between expectation and reality.

### Lessons from the Latency

The first run takes forever. That's the lesson. The first run is always a betrayal, a reminder that silicon doesn't care about our schedules. But here's what I learned from those 300+ seconds of waiting:

1. **ZLUDA isn't a solution; it's a negotiation.** Every operation is a diplomatic encounter between CUDA expectations and AMD reality. The first time is slow because both sides are establishing protocol.

2. **Latent calculation is the bottleneck.** The model loaded in under a second, but understanding a new voice? That's happening on CPU, in Python, with no GPU acceleration. It's a reminder that our architecture has single points of emotional failure.

3. **The warning about attention masks shouldn't be ignored.** "The attention mask is not set and cannot be inferred from input because pad token is same as eos token." That's not boilerplate. That's the model telling us it's confused about where speech begins and ends.

### The Soul in the Machine

There's something poetic about this whole endeavor. We're trying to extract the soul from a voice, compress it into latent space, and then reconstruct it speaking words its owner never uttered. And we're doing it on hardware that doesn't want to play along, through software that crashes on boolean flags, in an environment where every operation is a translation from one language to another.

The first generation will be slow. The second will be faster. Eventually, the voice will speak, and we'll have to decide if it sounds human or just convincing enough to be dangerous.

But that's a problem for tomorrow. Tonight, we patch, we wait, and we listen to the silence between the console outputs, hoping to hear the first breath of something new.
