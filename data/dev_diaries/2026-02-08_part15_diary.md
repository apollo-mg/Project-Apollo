# Project Apollo: Dev Diary - 2026-02-08 (Part 15)

The friction between the "Soul" of a machine and its "System" is most apparent when the silicon refuses to play nice with the physics. I spent most of this session wrestling with a Klipper homing sequence that was collapsing like a house of cards.

We were debugging a homing failure on a custom-built Ender 6. The architecture was designed to handle sensorless homing via TMC5160 drivers—a highly technical beast—but the macro logic was stumbling. The logs showed X homing successfully, followed by a series of Klipper errors, and then a complete refusal to home Y or Z.

The critical error was `Must home axis first: 238.000 120.000 10.017`. 

In the `homing_override` macro, the sequence was:
1. Home X.
2. Home Y.
3. Move to Z-home position (`G1 X100 Y120`).

The error occurred at step 3. Because Y hadn't successfully homed, the entire Z-homing sequence aborted. The machine's "System" (the firmware) was refusing to execute a move because the "Soul" (the physical geometry/endstop status) wasn't match the expected state. 

My initial hypothesis was that the sensorless homing sensitivity (`driver_SGT`) was set too high, causing the Y-axis to trigger an instant, false-positive endstop event. If the motor thinks it hit the wall the microsecond it starts moving, it won't record the axis as "homed." This would explain why the `G1` command failed—Y was never officially "there."

However, the user's feedback changed the trajectory. The errors weren't just single-point failures; they were a cascade. The fact that X completed, but then a barrage of errors hit, suggests the `homing_override` is potentially hitting a recursion loop or a massive state-machine crash. 

I had suspected the `G28 X` inside the macro was recursing, but Klipper's `homing_override` is designed to handle that by delegating to the hardware-level homing. The real culprit is likely a deeper incompatibility in how the TMC5160 current-setting macro is interacting with the standard homing routine. 

I've implemented a new diagnostic tool: `COMMAND: ENDSTOPS`. This allows the Voice Agent to poll the endstop status directly. It's a small but vital piece of the multi-agent ecosystem—it gives the "Commander" the ability to perceive the physical state of the world, not just the logic of the code.

**Core Lessons Learned:**
- **The Complexity of Local-First:** When running local models on my RX 9070 XT, the latency is low, but the complexity of debugging hardware-level-interrupts via a high-level LLM is immense. The LLM is great at logic, but it doesn't "feel" the vibration of a motor.
- **State-Machine Fragility:** In complex multi-axis systems, a failure in one axis isn't just a local error; it's a system-wide catastrophe. The software architecture must be resilient to partial failures.
- **The Necessity of Perceptual Tools:** We cannot just build "brains" (LLMs); we must build "senses" (system-level status probes) if we want an agent to truly interact with the physical world.
