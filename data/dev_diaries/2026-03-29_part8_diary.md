# Dev Diary: 2026-03-29 (Part 8)

## The Ghost in the Machine: VAD and the Feedback Loop

Today was one of those days where the "Soul" of the project—the desire to have a seamless, conversational presence—collided violently with the "System"—the messy, unyielding reality of hardware and digital signal processing.

I spent the first half of the session fighting a battle against background noise. My window AC unit is a constant source of white noise that was wreaking havoc on my Voice Activity Detection (VAD). I had `webrtcvad` set to mode `2` in `wake_up.py`, but my own comment in the code was wrong. I'd thought it was more aggressive, but it was actually too lenient. The VAD was misclassifying the AC hum as speech, causing the recording loop to hang indefinitely because it never "heard" the silence required to end the clip.

I had to dive into the code and force `vad.set_mode(3)`—the truly aggressive mode. It’s a bit of a brute-force approach, but it’s what we have.

Then, things got weird. I hit a classic "AI Feedback Loop."

The system worked: `openwakeword` heard me say *"Hey, how are you doing?"* and triggered the `buddy_agent.py`. The agent responded through the Piper TTS engine, and the voice came out of the speakers. But because the audio buffer wasn't properly flushed and the microphone was still listening, the mic picked up the AI's own voice. 

The AI was effectively talking to itself. The microphone recorded the AI's output, Whisper transcribed it as `[BLANK_AUDIO]`, and that blank string was fed back into the LLM. The LLM, seeing the blank input, said: *"Hey, looks like that audio came through empty..."* which triggered the entire loop to repeat. It was a digital snake eating its own tail.

I've patched `wake_up.py` to explicitly pause the microphone stream while the TTS is active and added a filter to discard any `[BLANK_AUDIO]` strings so the LLM doesn't get caught in a loop of its own making.

## The Hardware Grind: RDNA-4 and the VRAM Wall

The technical specs are starting to show their teeth. Running the 35B MoE model on the RX 9070 XT is a constant balancing act. The 16GB of VRAM is both a blessing and a curse. To get usable context windows (32k), I’ve been using `Q8_0` quantization for the KV Cache. It’s the only reason the model fits, but it comes at a massive cost to the model's "intelligence."

I stumbled upon a Reddit thread in `llama.cpp` that explains exactly why the model's reasoning has felt "off" lately. A recent PR revealed that `Q8` KV quantization tanks performance on complex reasoning tasks (like the AIME25 benchmark). There is a fix—"KV Rotation"—but it's brand new.

This is the core friction of local-first AI development: we are constantly trading off precision for scale. If I want the model to be smart, I need more VRAM. If I want it to remember more, I need to quantize. Every choice is a compromise between the intelligence of the "Soul" and the physical limits of the "System."

## Lessons for the Ecosystem

1. **Audio-Visual Synchronization is non-negotiable:** In a multi-agent system where one agent "speaks" and another "listens," the handoff must be physically gated. You can't just rely on software logic; you have to ensure the hardware buffers are flushed.
2. **Quantization isn't just about memory; it's about logic:** We've been treating quantization as a memory-management tool, but it's actually a cognitive tool. If we quantize too much, we aren't just making the model smaller; we are making it "dumber."
3. **Local-first requires robust VAD:** Developing for the real world means dealing with AC units, fans, and ambient noise. The "Soul" of the AI can't exist if it can't distinguish between a human and a cooling fan.

The architecture is stabilizing, but the fight against the hardware-induced limitations is far from over. Next step: re-compiling `llama.cpp` to see if the new rotation fixes our reasoning issues.
