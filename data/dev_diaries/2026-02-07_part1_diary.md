# Project Apollo Dev Diary: 2026-02-07

The friction between physical hardware and digital logic is never as apparent as when you're trying to map a 3D reality into a 2D coordinate system. Today was a grueling lesson in the "Soul vs. System" struggle. I spent most of the morning wrestling with the geometry of the Tri-Z Kinematic Bed, and by the time I hit the afternoon, the software was fighting me as much as the metal was.

## The Kinematic Mapping Nightmare

The core of the challenge is the `z_tilt` configuration. We are dealing with a complex mechanical assembly where the bed rests on three ball bearings, but the actual vertical force is applied by GE5C spherical bearings on the lead screws. 

The math is deceptively simple but unforgiving. I was trying to calculate the nozzle's `points` based on the bearing locations, but Klipper needs to know exactly where the *nozzle* is when the *sensor* hits the bearing. Because the BDsensor has a constant Y-offset, every coordinate shift is a potential failure point.

I had a moment of tension with the Gemini CLI. I was pushing for a specific coordinate calculation, and for a second, it lost the thread between the probe position and the nozzle position. I had to step in and remind the system: **the sensor is not the nozzle.** 

We eventually locked down the `z_positions` (the physical pivots) and the `points` (the nozzle's location during probing). It’s a delicate dance of offset math. One wrong sign on a Y-axis shift and the entire bed tilt calibration fails, leaving the bed skewed and useless.

## Mechanical Surgery & The Y-Axis Drift

After the math was settled, it was time for the "physical" part of the project. The lead screws needed cutting to accommodate the new travel limits. 

There was a tense moment regarding the Y-axis travel. I wanted to push the limits to gain more bed area, but the rail carrier was threatening to jump the track. We had to back off—subtracting 2mm of travel to ensure the carriage stays safely on the rail. It’s a reminder that even in a world of high-precision AI, you cannot ignore the hard limits of the physical world. The "System" (the rail) dictates the "Soul" (the potential of the build).

## The Internal Error: A Descent into Macro Hell

As the day wound down, the "System" started screaming. A catastrophic "Internal error on command: Z_TILT_ADJUST." 

This is where the beauty of the local-first AI architecture meets the messy reality of Python-based firmware. The command was failing mid-execution. I suspect a race condition or a malformed macro call between `printer.cfg` and `macros.cfg`. 

The debugging process was a descent into the file system. I had to pull the actual files from the Raspberry Pi (10.0.0.83) because the local versions were out of sync—a classic problem when working with remote-hosted local AI. The "Internal Error" is likely a Python crash triggered by the `Z_TILT_ADJUST` macro, potentially because it's trying to call a `BDsensor` routine that hasn't been properly initialized or has a scope error in the macro definition.

I've successfully recovered the configuration and the `input_shaper` data (X: 80Hz, Y: 48.2Hz) is intact, but the Z-tilt is currently a broken mess in the eyes of the firmware.

## Lessons for the Ecosystem

1. **Geometric Precision is Non-Negotiable:** In multi-agent systems that control hardware, the "ground truth" isn't just a data point; it's a physical coordinate. If the AI doesn't understand the offset of the sensor vs. the nozzle, the entire physical build is compromised.
2. **The Sync Problem is Real:** Working with local-first AI on a remote device (the Pi) introduces a latency and synchronization nightmare. You cannot rely on the local cache; you must treat the remote hardware as the only source of truth.
3. **The Fragility of Macros:** As we build more complex, autonomous agent-driven behaviors (like the `Z_TILT_ADJUST` routine), the complexity of the macro-dependency tree increases exponentially. One error in a macro can bring down the entire firmware stack.

**Status:** Hardware is cut. Y-travel is stable at 320mm. Z-tilt is broken. 
**Next Move:** Dig into `macros.cfg` to find the ghost in the machine.
