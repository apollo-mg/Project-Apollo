# Dev Diary: 2026-01-05
## The Lever Arm and the Aluminum Spine

Today was a brutal lesson in the difference between "mathematically perfect" and "physically robust." 

I spent the morning deep in the resonance data for the X and Y axes after the bed-rail modifications. The software—Gemini—was doing a beautiful job of analyzing the FFT graphs. The X-axis is a dream; the bedding made it so stiff that the resonance frequency jumped to nearly 150Hz, allowing us to push the acceleration limits into the stratosphere (~23k mm/s²) without the vibration eating the print quality. 

But the Y-axis... the Y-axis is a nightmare. 

The stiffness in the X-axis seems to have transferred a weird structural tension to the Y-frame, shifting the resonance peak up to 75Hz and making it incredibly sharp. We tried to surgically target it with an `mzv` shaper to maintain 16k acceleration, but as soon as I actually ran the machine, the truth came out. The "Soul" of the machine—the elegant, high-speed intent—was being crushed by the "System"—the physical reality of my printed parts.

The hotend is wobbling. It’s not just a vibration; it’s a literal mechanical deflection. I realized I’ve built a lever arm. The Goliath hotend is a massive, long beast, and it's mounted to a tall, printed EVA-style structure. Every time the Y-axis accelerates, that long plastic assembly is acting like a lever, twisting the whole assembly. The Input Shaper sees a "clean" graph because the vibration is happening at a frequency the software thinks it has handled, but the physical torque is bending the plastic frame like it’s made of jelly.

I hit a wall. The software is perfect, the tuning is perfect, but the material science is failing. 

I was considering a full CNC overhaul—moving to a VzBot CNC printhead—but that's too much friction. It’s too much change to the ecosystem (6mm belts, different pathing, etc.). I need a scalpel, not a sledgehammer.

The epiphany hit: I don't need to replace the whole toolhead. I just need to stiffen the spine.

I have a 100x100x5mm aluminum plate sitting on my workbench. It’s sitting there, ignored, while the real solution. If I can turn that plate into a rigid top-plate, I can bridge the gap between the linear rail carriage and the hotend mount, effectively turning the "lever" into a rigid, short-stature unit. 

The problem? I don't have a drill press. Drilling 2.5mm holes for M3 taps through 5mm of aluminum with a handheld drill is a recipe for a stripped thread and a broken tap. It’s a high-stress, high-precision task with low-precision tools.

But I am a developer. I have a 3D printer. 

I’m going to design a drill guide jig. A sacrificial plastic block with the exact hole pattern. I'll press some hardened metal tube (maybe some old heatbreak material) into the jig to act as a bushing, clamp it to the aluminum, and then drill. If the jig keeps the bit perpendicular, I can beat the physics of the handheld drill.

It’s a messy, manual, low-tech solution to a high-tech problem, but it's the only way to bridge the gap between the Silicon (the tuning) and the Silicon (the physical toolhead) without spending a fortune.

- Y-Axis: Managed but unstable (mzv @ 74.0Hz)
- Toolhead: Pending "The Aluminum Spine" operation.

Tomorrow, we see if the metal can handle the torque.
