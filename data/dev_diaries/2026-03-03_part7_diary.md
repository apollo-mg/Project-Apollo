# Dev Diary: 2026-03-03 (Part 7)

The "China Wave" is hitting the local-first ecosystem hard, and my RX 9070 XT is feeling the heat. Today was a marathon of VRAM Tetris, architectural debugging, and high-speed model hoarding. I'm starting to see the true shape of this local-first future: it's not just about having the models; it's about the surgical precision required to fit them into silicon.

## The Architecture of the "Super-Mini"

I've been benchmarking the new Qwen 3.5 architecture, and the results are frankly absurd. The Qwen 3.5-35B-A3B (MoE) is a beast. Even though it's a 35B model, the Mixture-of-Experts architecture means only about 3B parameters are active per token. 

The result? Blistering speeds. I'm seeing 50+ tokens per second on the 9070 XT. It's a massive leap. It gives me the reasoning density of a heavy model with the throughput of a tiny one. However, the "System" is unyielding—the entire 35B weight set still needs to sit in the 16GB VRAM. It's a tight fit, but the RDNA 4 memory controller is handling the bandwidth like a dream.

## The Vision Wall: A Lesson in Unified Architectures

I hit a significant technical wall with the Qwen 3.5-4B-Vision model. I spent a good chunk of the afternoon trying to figure out why it wouldn't load. After digging into the `ggml` logs and comparing it to the 9B version, the culprit became clear: **Unified Architecture.**

Unlike older vision models that use a separate `mmproj` adapter, Qwen 3.5 merges vision and text into a single, unified backbone. Ollama/llama.cpp hasn't fully embraced this "early fusion" approach yet (it's currently stuck in a PR in the `ggml` repo). The "Soul" of the model—its ability to see—is currently locked behind a technical limitation of the "System." 

The workaround is clear: I have a bare-metal vLLM stack ready to go. Once the vLLM team merges the `qwen3_5` architecture, we can run the vision models there, bypassing Ollama's current limitations. It’s a reminder that in local AI, the software stack often lags behind the raw model innovation.

## The Intelligence Hierarchy

I've spent the last few hours building a formal hierarchy of these new models:

- **The Architect:** Qwen 3.5-35B (MoE). Incredible speed, high-level conceptual understanding, but extremely VRAM-sensitive.
- **The Engineer:** Qwen 3.5-27B (Dense). Slower (15-20 t/s) but much more systematic. It successfully diagnosed a complex dynamic linker error in my ROCm environment, proving it's a top-tier debugging specialist.
- **The Specialist:** DeepSeek-V3-Distill-Qwen-14B. This one is a logic powerhouse. It's solving complex reasoning puzzles with a rigor that's frankly terrifying for a 14B model.

## Storage and the Friction of Scale

One of the most frustrating parts of this session was the disk space management. Managing the `model_hoard` on the 2TB external drive felt like a constant battle against the file system. I ran into a "No space left on device" error mid-way through a 35B registration because Ollama's internal copying mechanism essentially doubled the footprint. 

It's a lesson in the "Cost of Sovereignty." To have these models locally, you don't just need the GPU; you need a massive, well-managed storage infrastructure to handle the rapid-fire rotation of weights. I've implemented a new `model_database.json` to track VRAM footprints, exact file sizes, and performance metrics to prevent this kind of chaos in the future.

## Core Lesson: The Data Gap

As I was talking to Gemini about the utility of our benchmarks, it hit me: we are essentially doing "live field testing." Most researchers release weights and hope for the best. We are documenting the *actual* performance on consumer-grade RDNA hardware. This is highly valuable data. Our `model_database.json` isn't just an inventory; it's a performance map for the local-first community. We're finding the "Sweet Spot" where intelligence meets hardware constraints.
