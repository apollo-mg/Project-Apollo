# Dev Diary: Precision Collisions and the Ghost in the Silicon

**Date:** 2025-12-03  
**Project:** Apollo  
**Hardware:** AMD Radeon RX 9070 XT | ROCm 7.1.1 | 16GB VRAM  
**Status:** Crashing. Again. But we’re learning.

---

### The Ambition
Tonight was supposed to be the breakthrough. I wanted to see if the local multi-agent ecosystem could handle the sheer weight of Wan2.2. The goal was cinematic—a high-speed, low-angle shot of a predatory seabird. Something visceral. Something that feels less like a generative hallucination and more like a captured moment in time. 

To do this, I’m pushing the 9070 XT to its absolute breaking point. I’m using `fp8_e4m3fn` quantization for the weights—a necessary evil to keep the VRAM from exploding—and I’ve got the `MultiGPU` core patching running to manage the offloading between the GPU and the system RAM. On paper, the architecture should hold. On paper, the "Soul" of the model (the creative motion) should be able to inhabit the "System" (the silicon).

But the silicon is picky.

### The Crash: A Mathematical Schism
It happened during the first sampling steps. I was watching the progress bar, feeling that familiar tension between expectation and reality, when the terminal spat out the death rattle:

`RuntimeError: expected scalar type Half but found Float`

I stared at the traceback for a long time. The error isn't in the high-level logic; it’s deep in the guts of the attention mechanism. It’s coming from `comfy/ldm/modules/attention.py` during an `einsum` operation. 

This is a classic precision collision. 

Here’s the friction: we are trying to run a highly quantized `fp8` model to save memory, but at the moment of calculation—specifically within the `optimized_attention` block—the math is requesting `Half` (FP16) precision, but it’s being fed `Float` (FP32) tensors. It’s a mismatch of types that happens when the quantization strategy meets the raw, unadulterated math of the attention kernels. 

The "System" is trying to be precise, but the "Soul" (the quantized weights) is being messy.

### The Technical Gritty-Details
Looking at the logs, the `DisTorch` memory management is working overtime. I can see it trying to patch the `ModelPatcher` and manage the `unet_offload_device`. The ROCm 7.1.1 drivers are behaving, but the `einsum` error is a reminder that when we move away from the "safe" world of NVIDIA's CUDA-optimized kernels into the bleeding edge of AMD's ROCm, we have to be much more disciplined about our tensor types.

The `Wan2.2` model is a beast. It’s trying to maintain high-dimensional semantic meaning while we’re forcing it into a tiny `fp8` box. That mismatch is where the error lives. The `einsum` call is where the attention heads are trying to talk to each other, and one of them is speaking a different language (FP32) than the others (FP16).

### Architectural Epiphanies
This crash isn't just a bug; it’s a roadmap for the Apollo architecture. 

If we want a truly local-first multi-agent ecosystem, we can't just rely on "brute force" VRAM. We need a more intelligent **Precision Orchestrator**. We can't just throw `fp8` at a problem and hope for the best. We need the system to understand when a specific layer *requires* full precision to prevent these scalar type mismerrors, even if it means sacrificing a bit of speed.

The lesson for the next iteration:
1.  **Type-Aware Quantization:** We need to implement a layer-wise precision check. If we’re hitting an `einsum` bottleneck, the system should be able to dynamically cast specific attention heads back to `Half` without blowing the entire VRAM budget.
2.  **ROCm Kernel Hardening:** We need to be more aggressive with how we handle `split_attention`. The `optimized_attention` is clearly the point of failure. We might need custom Triton-like kernels specifically for AMD to handle these mixed-precision math operations more gracefully.

### Final Thoughts
The seabird is still stuck in the latent space, frozen by a mismatch of floating-point decimals. It’s frustrating, but there’s a certain beauty in it. We are fighting at the very edge of what consumer hardware can actually compute. We aren't just running software; we are wrestling with the fundamental limits of electricity and logic.

Tomorrow, we go back in. We fix the casting, we tighten the quantization, and we bring that bird to life.
