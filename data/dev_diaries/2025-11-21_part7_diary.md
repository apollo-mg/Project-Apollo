## The Wall of Memory

Sixteen gigabytes.

That's the promise the silicon merchant sold me. Sixteen glorious gigabytes of VRAM on the RX 9070 XT, gleaming like a cathedral window in the dark room of my server rack.

I asked it for six hundred and fifty-eight megabytes.

It laughed in my face.

The error message scrolled up the terminal like an obituary: `HIP out of memory. Tried to allocate 658.00 MiB. GPU 0 has a total capacity of 15.92 GiB of which 252.00 MiB is free.`

Two hundred and fifty-two megabytes free. Two hundred and fifty-two. I had room for a single JPEG thumbnail. I did not have room for a single step of Euler noise through a fourteen-billion-parameter flow model.

The arithmetic was insulting in its simplicity. PyTorch had allocated fourteen point four two gigabytes. The model itself—quantized to Q4_K, mercy on my soul—had loaded at six point four gigabytes. The T5 XXL text encoder sat on the CPU, supposedly safe. But somewhere in the gap between what was loaded and what was allocated, a ghost had entered the room.

---

## The Lie of expandable_segments

The error message, in its infinite bureaucratic mercy, offered me a lifeline:

> *If reserved but unallocated memory is large try setting PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True to avoid fragmentation.*

So I did. I set the environment variable. I restarted ComfyUI. I watched the logs scroll by like prayers to a god who wasn't listening.

Then came the warning, nestled innocently between two FETCH operations:

```
UserWarning: expandable_segments not supported on this platform 
(Triggered internally at /pytorch/c10/hip/HIPAllocatorConfig.h:36.)
```

That's when I felt it—the first real spike of fury in this project.

CUDA developers got their pretty allocator features. CUDA developers got their expandable segments, their garbage collection thresholds, their fine-grained memory management. ROCm? We got a warning message and a shrug from the PyTorch maintainers.

The AMD driver was looking at my `PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF` and doing what AMD does best: politely ignoring it while pretending to be helpful.

## The RoPE That Bound Me

The crash happened here, in the bones of the positional encoding:

```python
# comfy/ldm/flux/math.py:83
return x_out.reshape(*x.shape).to(dtype=orig_dtype)
```

That `.to(dtype=orig_dtype)` was the knife.

The tensor arrived in BF16—brain float, they call it, as if it were some neurological breakthrough rather than a memory hack. The RoPE computation needed float32 precision for stability. So we upcast. We compute. We downcast back.

But somewhere in that dance, the memory allocator choked. The reshape created a view. The `.to()` created a copy. The copy needed to breathe, and the GPU had no air left to give.

I watched the stack trace like a coroner examining a wound:

```
File "/comfy/ldm/wan/model.py", line 78, in forward
    q = qkv_fn_q(x)
File "/comfy/ldm/wan/model.py", line 69, in qkv_fn_q
    return apply_rope1(q, freqs)
File "/comfy/ldm/flux/math.py", line 83, in apply_rope1
    return x_out.reshape(*x.shape).to(dtype=orig_dtype)
```

Three files. Three layers of abstraction. Three promises that someone, somewhere, had thought about memory management.

## The Architecture of Compromise

Here's what I learned today, carved into the granite of my frustration:

**The Soul wants to generate video.** The Soul wants a cinematic drone shot of a futuristic city at sunset, neon lights bleeding into wet asphalt, flying cars carving arcs through volumetric clouds. The Soul wants thirty-three frames at 960x544, flowing together like water over stone.

**The System has sixteen gigabytes.** The System has a quantized fourteen-billion-parameter model that doesn't fit in memory when you ask it to think about time. The System has a T5 XXL encoder that refuses to die even when you tell it to move to CPU. The System has a VAE that wants to decode latents faster than the VRAM can clear them.

The friction between these two realities is where I live now.

---

## The Fragmentation Problem

The memory report told a story:

- **Total VRAM:** 15.92 GiB
- **PyTorch Allocated:** 14.42 GiB  
- **PyTorch Reserved but Unallocated:** 815.73 MiB
- **Free:** 252.00 MiB

Eight hundred and fifteen megabytes reserved but *unallocated*. That's the fragmentation gap—the space between what PyTorch thinks it owns and what it can actually use. The allocator had carved the VRAM into Swiss cheese, and I was trying to pour milk through it.

The `expandable_segments` feature would have coalesced these fragments. But ROCm doesn't have it. So I'm left with options:

1. **Lower the resolution** — Betray the Soul's vision for clarity
2. **Reduce the frame count** — Sacrifice temporal coherence
3. **Aggressive offloading** — Accept the CPU as a permanent resident
4. **Rewrite the RoPE** — Fight the math itself

None of them feel like victory.

---

## The Path Forward

I'm trying `max_split_size_mb:128` in the allocator configuration. It's a gamble—forcing the allocator to be more conservative about chunk sizes, hoping that smaller fragments mean more predictable coalescing.

I'm considering chunked RoPE computation. Process the sequence in pieces. Accept the overhead of multiple passes through the attention mechanism. Trade compute for memory, because compute I have in abundance.

The real lesson, though, is architectural:

**The local-first dream requires acceptance of constraints.**

The cloud doesn't have these problems. The cloud has 80GB H100s that laugh at fragmentation. The cloud has memory pools that span racks. The cloud has money.

I have sixteen gigabytes and a hatred of OOM errors.

But there's something beautiful in the constraint. Something honest. When I solve this, when I get that drone shot rendering at 16 frames per second on bare metal, it won't be because I rented someone else's silicon. It'll be because I understood the silicon I own well enough to bend it.
