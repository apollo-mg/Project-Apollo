# Apollo Dev Diary: The Vibration Paradox & The Structural Pivot

**Date:** 2026-02-24
**Session:** Part 1 of 2

Today was a masterclass in the friction between elegant software and messy, physical reality. I spent the first half of the day battling the ghost in the machine—literally.

### The Ghost in the Gantry
The new Aluminum 2020 extrusion was supposed to be the "fix" for the Y-axis twist that was ruining my precision. I thought we had conquered the mechanical resonance. I was wrong. 

The `VIBRATIONS_CALIBRATION` run was a brutal, beautiful moment of truth. The ShakeTune output was a revelation. It didn't just show noise; it showed a massive, rhythmic mountain of vibration peaking at 105 mm/s. My LDO motors have a specific, nasty "twang" at 256 Hz, and when the machine hits that speed, the whole frame enters a sympathetic resonance that turns a precision instrument into a vibrating tuning fork.

It’s a bizarre feeling. The software is doing its job perfectly—detecting the axes, mapping the sensor, and quantifying the exact frequency of the mechanical failure. The "Soul" (the goal of a rock-solid, high-speed printer) is hitting the wall of the "System" (the physical resonance of the motor/frame combo).

### The Speed Limit Epiphany
We aren't limited by how fast the motors *can* go, but by how fast they *should* go to avoid destroying the print quality. The vibration map has effectively carved out a "Danger Zone" between 75 and 125 mm/s. 

The solution is counter-intuitive: I have to print *faster* to be faster. I have to skip the middle ground entirely. By pushing the infill to 150+ mm/s, I bypass the resonance. I’m basically jumping over the problem. It’s a weird, high-performance way of thinking—trading a midrange "sweet spot" for a high-speed "green zone."

### The Software Wall: The SGT/Autotune Conflict
The most frustrating part of the day wasn't the physics, but the software architecture. I was trying to tune the sensorless homing sensitivity, thinking the issue was a simple parameter tweak in `printer.cfg`. 

Turns out, the `klipper-tmc-autotune` module is a bit of a tyrant. It’s a hard-coded overwrite. It ignores the user's `driver_SGT` settings in the main config and applies its own defaults during the autotuning process. It’s a classic case of a "black box" subsystem overriding the "open" configuration. It’s a reminder that when building local-first multi-agent systems, you can't just assume the underlying drivers respect your intent—you have to find the specific entry point for the sub-modules.

### Lessons for the Ecosystem
1. **Hardware is the ultimate bottleneck:** No amount of quantization or clever math can save a print from a structural resonance. 
2. **The "Skip the Midrange" Strategy:** In high-performance systems, sometimes the optimal path is a "leap" rather than a gradual climb.
3. **Software Friction:** When working with modularized AI/Firmware, always check the autotune/calibration modules first. They are the silent usurpers of your manual settings.

The machine is now a beast. It’s loud, it’s fast, and it’s incredibly rigid. The aluminum swap worked. The data is clean. The next step is seeing if this beast can actually handle the flow rate we're about to throw at it.
