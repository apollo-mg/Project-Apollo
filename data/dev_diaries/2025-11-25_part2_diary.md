Dev Diary - 2025-11-25 (Part 2)

The Soul wanted to hear my voice again. But the System—the RX 9070 XT, all 16 GB of it—wasn't cooperating.

It began with a `ModuleNotFoundError` for `faster_whisper`, then a `CUDA driver version is insufficient` error that shouldn't have happened on an AMD card. The script was looking for NVIDIA CUDA when I had HIP/ROCm staring it in the face. Gemini and I pored over the code. We found the culprit: `ctranslate2`, the engine behind the Whisper ASR, doesn't support ROCm out of the box. It was defaulting to CUDA and choking.

We patched `fasterwhisper_asr.py` to force `device="cpu"`. But then came another snag: `float16` on CPU. The error was elegant—`Requested float16 compute type, but the target device or backend do not support efficient float16 computation`. Of course. The CPU doesn't do native FP16 the way the GPU does. We switched the precision to `float32` and let the ASR run its course. It was slower, but stable.

Then, finally, the training began. A flood of MIOpen warnings about workspace buffers. I ignored them—noise, not error. The terminal scrolled: `[3.48, 2.79, 14.03...]`—the losses trending down. Epoch 1, Epoch 2.

Gemini checked the VRAM: 9.3 GB used. Of the 16. That left me room to breathe, but not enough to be reckless. Batch size 4 was the sweet spot. Not 8, not 16. Just 4. Safe. Stable.

This is the work of local-first. It is not the cloud where the hardware disappears into a black box. Here, every ROCm driver version and every float bit counts. The friction is the point.

Lessons for the ecosystem:
- Hardware specificity matters. ROCm is not CUDA.
- Quantization (float16 vs float32) is a system boundary you must respect.
- When you train locally, you trade speed for control. And sometimes, you trade batch size for memory.

The training will finish. The inference tab waits. I can almost hear the synthesized voice—my voice—returning.
