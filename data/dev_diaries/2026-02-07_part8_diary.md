# Dev Diary: 2026-02-07 | The Mirror and the Machine

The friction between the "Soul" and the "System" is real. Today was a battle against memory fragmentation, VRAM bottlenecks, and the stubborn, pedantic walls of Windows security. 

The morning was spent wrestling with the RX 9070 XT. I was pushing the limits of the KV cache, trying to get the model to ingest a massive 16k-token `printer.cfg` file. The system was choking; the initial attempt hit a 120-second hang that felt like staring into a void of frozen silicon. I felt that familiar dread—the feeling that the local architecture is too heavy for the hardware's current driver state.

But then, the breakthrough. After fine-tuning the context limits and the cache, the "hang" collapsed into a 49.7-second success. The local agent didn't just work; it *saw*. It processed the entire config and spit out a technical diff in under a minute. The "Tier 3" dream is real. The local-first architecture isn't just a theory; it's a functioning reality. My hardware can now hold the entire "truth" of the machine in its head at once.

Then came the networking nightmare.

The goal was simple: Give the AI a way to see the Raspberry Pi without giving it the keys to the kingdom. I proposed the "Mirror" architecture—a read-only bridge where the AI only sees what the Pi "pushes" to a local folder. 

But Windows is a paranoid host. It blocked my attempts at guest access via Samba, citing "insecure guest logons." I had to dive into the Linux-to-Windows bridge, battling Samba's internal credential system. It was a mess of `smb.conf` edits and permission errors. I even accidentally locked myself out of `sudo` while trying to clean up the `pi` user's permissions. 

It was a humbling moment of "AI friction"—the agent was trying to fix its own environment but was hitting the very security walls it was trying to build. After a frantic session of re-configuring the `gemini` user and injecting a new SSH key, I finally cracked it. The "Mirror" is live. The Windows machine now sees the Pi's config as a local drive. The air-gap is established. The AI can read the "Ground Truth" of the printer, but it can't touch a single pin on the hardware unless I say so.

The architectural vision is crystallizing. We aren't just building a chatbot; we are building a **Swarm**.

The 9070 XT is a heavy-duty engine, but it's not infinite. I'm thinking about a "Task Router" approach. I want a "Watchdog" (a tiny Phi-4 model) running constantly in the background with minimal VRAM footprint, watching the logs. If it detects a crash, it triggers a "Vision" agent (Llava) to look at the camera. If the vision agent sees spaghetti, it triggers the "Engineer" (DeepSeek) to analyze the thermal logs and suggest a new cooling profile.

The hardware is the body, the LLMs are the brain, and the Python/PowerShell scripts are the nervous system. Right now, the nervous system is being wired, and the brain is starting to wake up.
