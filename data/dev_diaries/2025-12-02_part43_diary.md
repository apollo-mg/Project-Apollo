# Dev Diary: The Silicon Ceiling and the Ghost in the Tiles

**December 2, 2025 | 04:30 AM**
**Location: Local Dev Station | Hardware: AMD RX 9070 XT | Project: Apollo**

Tonight was a war of attrition between the creative intent of the Wan 2.1 model and the brutal, uncompromising physics of RDNA 4 memory management. We aren't just building a pipeline; we’re trying to orchestrate a symphony on a medium that wants to scream.

### The Ghost in the Machine: Spatial Tiling and the Flickering Seams

The session started with a visual nightmare: flickering. We were running the Wan 2.1 VAE, and the output was jittery, a rhythmic shimmering that felt less like "AI magic" and more like a hardware error. 

Gemini and I went down the rabbit hole of the `WanVideoVAE` implementation. The culprit wasn't the weights or the quantization; it was the geometry. We’re using spatial tiling to keep VRAM from exploding, but we were being too stingy with the tile size. By setting the tiles at 128x128, we were essentially cutting the image into tiny, microscopic shards. 

The VAE has a massive "Receptive Field"—it needs to see a wide context to make sense of a pixel. When we slice the latent space into such small chunks, the convolution padding at the edges of the tiles creates "border artifacts." Normally, our blending logic (`build_1d_mask`) tries to smooth these seams, but because the tiles are so small, the "safe" center of the tile is too narrow. When motion enters the frame, those edge artifacts dance across the screen. 

**The Lesson:** The "Soul" of the video—the motion—is being strangled by the "System" limits. If we want stability, we have to push the tile size up to 256 or 324. It’s a trade-off: higher VRAM pressure for a coherent image, or small tiles and a shimmering mess.

### The "Nuclear Option" Backfire: Memory Thrashing

Then things got ugly. The system started hitting a "one-and-done" pattern. We’d run a generation, it would look beautiful, and then... *silence*. The server would stall during the next model load. Total deadlock.

We thought it was a VRAM leak, but it was actually something much more insidious: **Swap Thrashing.**

To stabilize the RDNA 4 drivers, we had implemented a "Nuclear Option" patch. To prevent the driver from losing its mind during massive weight transfers, we were forcing every weight tensor through a `numpy.copy()` before sending it to the GPU. We thought we were being safe. In reality, we were being reckless. 

For a 14B parameter model, that `numpy.copy()` was creating a massive, temporary duplicate of the entire model in System RAM. We were essentially doubling the memory footprint of the model during the H2D (Host-to-Device) transfer. The OS couldn't handle it. It hit the swap file, the disk latency skyrocketed, and the whole machine choked on its own weight.

### The Optimization: Returning to the Metal

We had to strip back the complexity. We replaced the heavy Numpy overhead with a more surgical PyTorch approach: `tensor.clone().contiguous()`. 

It’s a subtle distinction, but it’s everything. By staying within the PyTorch allocation scheme, we avoid the massive RAM spike of the Numpy conversion while still giving the driver the contiguous memory blocks it needs to avoid a crash. It’s about working *with* the silicon, not fighting against it.

### The RDNA 4 Reality Check

We also had to gut the `expandable_segments` flag. It turns out that "feature" is a bit of a lie on this architecture; it was causing more instability than it was worth. We’re also leaning heavily on `--reserve-vram`. On a desktop Linux setup, if you let the AI take every single byte, your display driver dies and you're left staring at a black screen. We need that 2GB buffer just to keep the lights on.

**Current State of the Architecture:**
*   **Memory Strategy:** Aggressive offloading via `LOW_VRAM` state. We're moving weights to CPU immediately after use. It’s slow, but it’s the only way to play in this sandbox.
*   **Tiling Strategy:** Shifting toward larger spatial tiles to combat the "flicker" artifacts.
*   **Optimization Status:** Moving from "brute force" stability to "surgical" memory management.

We’re getting there. It’s messy, it’s loud, and it’s incredibly technical, but we’re finally beginning to understand the limits of the machine.

**Next up: Implementing temporal tiling. If we can solve the memory footprint of the time-dimension, we win.**
