# Project Apollo: Dev Diary - 2026-01-21

The transition from "The Idea" to "The Reality" is rarely a straight line. Today was a brutal exercise in the friction between hardware-defined logic and software-defined intent. I spent most of the evening wrestling with the BTT Eddy sensor, a component that was supposed to be a simple upgrade but turned into a labyrinth of I2C protocol errors and configuration cascading failures.

### The Connection Dilemma

I started by evaluating the mounting of the Eddy. It’s a dual-mode device—USB or CAN. I initially wondered if I could bypass the complexity by plugging the USB-A output directly into the Octopus Pro’s USB-A port, hoping the board might bridge it as a CAN device. 

It was a dead end. The protocols are fundamentally incompatible; you can't just shove differential CAN signals into a USB transceiver without a dedicated bridge. I had to make a call: use the USB mode via the Raspberry Pi for immediate gratification, or commit to the CAN bus complexity. I chose the path of least resistance (USB) to keep the cable management sane, planning to daisy-chain the CAN bus via the toolhead later. 

### The I2C Wall

Then, the silicon fought back. 

After flashing the new firmware, I hit a wall of `I2C START READ NACK` errors. This is the electronic equivalent of a "silent treatment." The RP2040 microcontroller on the Eddy was trying to talk to the LDC1612 sensor on the same board, and the sensor simply wasn't answering. 

I went down a rabbit hole of address-hunting. I tried `43` (the standard), then `42`, then `41`. None of them worked. The error wasn't just a mismatch; it was a fundamental breakdown in the I2C bus communication.

### The Config Cascade

The most frustrating part wasn't the hardware, but the software's intolerance. Klipper is a powerful engine, but it is incredibly rigid. Because I had commented out the `[probe_eddy_current]` section to bypass the I2C error, the entire configuration collapsed like a house of cards. 

The `[stepper_z]` was looking for a `probe` that didn't exist. The `[axis_twist_compensation]` was looking for a probe that didn't exist. It was a cascade of dependencies. I had to perform "surgery" on the `printer.cfg`, creating dummy endstop pins and fake-mapping GPIOs just to trick the system into a stable boot state so I could finally reach the console.

### The Diagnostic Dead-End

I finally achieved a stable boot—a small victory—but the `I2C_SCAN` command returned nothing. The bus is empty. 

This leaves me with three grim possibilities:
1. The physical switch on the Eddy is stuck in CAN mode while I'm using the USB port.
2. The internal I2C wiring on the board is faulty (bad batch?).
3. The firmware/I2C bus assignment is fundamentally mismatched with this specific hardware revision.

### Lessons for the Local-First Ecosystem

This session reminded me that even in a highly automated, local-AI-driven world, the "System" (the silicon) is unforgiving. You can have the most elegant architecture in the world, but if your I2C bus doesn't handshake, your "Soul" (the creative potential of the probe) is trapped behind a wall of error logs.

Tomorrow, I’ll be digging into the hardware itself. If the switch isn't the culprit, I'm going to have to re-evaluate the entire firmware build process. The local-first dream requires a level of technical grit that most "plug-and-play" users never encounter. 

**Status:** Frustrated, but the architecture is holding. Time for more coffee.
