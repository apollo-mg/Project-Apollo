# Project Apollo: Dev Diary - 2026-03-06 (Part 14)

The friction between the "Soul" and the "System" is reaching a fever pitch. I set out to test the raw power of the RX 9070 XT, but the silicon is a temperamental beast, and the filesystem is a fickle ghost.

Today was a lesson in the volatility of local-first ecosystems. We hit a massive hardware crash—an OOM (Out of Memory) event that didn't just kill the process; it corrupted the very drive holding our model weights. The 3TB archival drive hit an inconsistent state, its Master File Table Mirror ($MFTMirr) desynced from the primary MFT. Linux refused to mount it. We were trapped. Our most valuable assets—the large Flux weights—were essentially held hostage by a filesystem corruption.

But we didn't stop. We pivoted.

We realized that even with the 3TB drive dead, we could still validate the RDNA 4 architecture. I instructed the system to pull a smaller, 6.5GB SDXL-Turbo model onto the fast NVMe. It was a tactical retreat to ensure we could still verify the ROCm 7.2/PyTorch 2.12.0 stack. The results were staggering. Despite the small model, the GPU hit a peak clock of 3149 MHz. The hardware is incredibly fast—so fast, in fact, that the standard 1-second polling interval of `rocm-smi` actually missed the power spike entirely. It's a reminder that "speed" in local AI isn't just about throughput; it's about transient bursts that can outrun our monitoring tools.

Then came the architecture epiphany. We discussed the "Generative Agent"—a vision of a workflow where the AI isn't just a generator, but a closed-loop entity. A system that *thinks* via an LLM (9B Heretic), *forges* via Flux, and *audits* its own work via a VLM (Qwen3-VL). 

The technical hurdle? VRAM. On a 16GB card, this is a game of "Tetris." The LLM, the UNet, and the VLM cannot all live in the GPU at once. We had to implement a sophisticated "Dynamic Offloading" strategy, using the `--normalvram` flag to swap the T5 encoder in and out of System RAM. It's a delicate dance of memory management that turns a 16GB card into a 19GB powerhouse.

Finally, we addressed the underlying instability of the OS itself. The Ubuntu root partition was choking on a bloated `/tmp` directory. I've implemented a "Sovereign-Grade" hardening:
- **Hardware Locking:** We've moved away from the flimsy "Automounter" (which was causing the `AI_Fast` $\rightarrow$ `AI_Fast1` name drift) and locked the drives via their hardware UUIDs in `/etc/fstab`.
- **Partition Relief:** We've effectively moved the `/tmp` bottleneck to the 1.2TB drive via a bind mount. The root partition is finally breathing again.

The infrastructure is now ready. The "Agentic Lab" is live. The silicon is stable. The path is clear for the first autonomous loop.
