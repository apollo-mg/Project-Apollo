**Date:** 2025-12-03  
**Session:** Part 5 | The ROCm Frontier  
**Hardware:** AMD Radeon RX 9070 XT | 16GB VRAM | ROCm 7.1.1  

The dream of local-first, high-fidelity intelligence is starting to feel less like a software engineering project and more like a war of attrition against silicon.

Tonight was supposed to be the breakthrough. I was pushing the Wan2.1 13b model—a beast of a model—trying to see just how much "soul" we could extract from the quantization. I’ve been leaning heavily on GGUF resident blocks, trying to play this dangerous game of memory Tetris: moving weights into VRAM just in time for the attention heads to grab them, then offloading them before the next layer hits. It’s a delicate dance of high-speed memory management.

Then, the floor fell out.

### The Crash: `hipErrorIllegalAddress`

It happened at 40% through the sampling loop. One second, the progress bar was crawling steadily; the next, the terminal screamed.

`torch.AcceleratorError: HIP error: an most illegal memory access was encountered.`

In the NVIDIA world, you get a clean `Out of Memory` error. In the AMD world, you get a kernel-level violation. An "illegal memory access" is much more violent. It means the GPU tried to touch a memory address that didn't belong to it, or a pointer went rogue during a weight-casting operation. 

Looking at the stack trace, the culprit is staring me in the face: `comfy.model_management.cast_to`. The system was trying to move weights into the device (`weight.to(device, non_blocking=True)`), likely as part of the `lowvram` strategy. The ROCm driver, tasked with managing the high-speed shuffle of these GGUF blocks, hit a race condition. It tried to move a weight block that was either already being accessed or was partially corrupted by a previous kernel.

### The Friction: Soul vs. System

This is the classic dichotomy of Project Apollo. 

On one side, you have the **Soul**: the massive, multidimensional weights of the 13b model. It's an emergent, beautiful architecture capable of generating near-cinematic reality. It's what we are building for.

On the other side, you have the **System**: the cold, hard, brutal constraints of the 9070 XT. I have 16GB of VRAM to work with, but I'm trying to run a model that wants more. The `--lowvram` and `--reserve-vram 3.0` flags are my attempts to build a cage around the beast, but the cage is leaking.

The more we compress these models with quantization to make them fit, the more we strain the memory controller. We are essentially trying to squeeze a gallon of genius into a pint-sized silicon vessel. The "illegal memory access" is the sound of that vessel cracking.

### Technical Post-Mortem & Next Steps

The logs show the `GGUF_GPU_RESIDENT` patcher was working overtime, moving `blocks.37.self_attn` and `blocks.38.ffn` into place. The fragmentation must have been insane. We’re hitting a wall where the overhead of managing the memory is starting to corrupt the memory itself.

**The Plan:**

1.  **Kernel Serialization:** I need to run this with `AMD_SERIALIZE_KERNEL=3`. It’ll kill performance by killing parallelism, but I need to see if we can catch the exact moment the pointer goes rogue. Is it a driver bug or a genuine memory leak in the `lowvram` implementation?
2.  **Quantization Audit:** We might be pushing the GGUF bit-depth too hard. If the quantization kernels aren't perfectly optimized for the RDNA architecture in ROCm 7.1.1, we're asking for these illegal address errors.
3.  **Memory Padding:** I need to look at the `reserve-vram` logic. Maybe we aren't leaving enough "breathing room" for the intermediate activations during the `uni_pc` sampling step. The sampler is trying to be efficient, but efficiency is often the enemy of stability.

This is the price of entry. If we want to escape the NVIDIA monopoly and run real, high-parameter models locally, we have to learn to dance with the AMD driver. It’s unrefined, it’s temperamental, but when it works... it's pure magic.

**Status:** *Recovering. Recompiling. Pushing forward.*
