The VRAM Wars & The Stack That Overflowed

My RX 9070 XT is humming again, but it was a close call. Today was a deep dive into the gritty tension between the "Soul" of Project Apollo—these ambitious, autonomous agents—and the cold, hard limits of our silicon.

It started with a question of **TurboQuant**. We wanted to push that context window to the moon using KV Caching, but the architecture split right down the middle. Vulkan, with its beautiful cross-platform promise, didn't have the shader kernels written for the Turbo format. It was a silent fallback to the CPU, and my generation speed crated. I switched back to the ROCm build. The HIP kernels were there, waiting. Full PCIe speeds restored. Lesson learned: bleeding-edge tensor formats demand the backend that speaks their dialect.

Then came the **MoE** hunt. I'm testing that Qwopus-MoE-35B against the newer Gemopus from Jackrong. It's a classic VRAM trap. You want the 35B knowledge base, but you only have 16GB of VRAM to spare. I learned the hard way that you cannot simply load a dense quant. The `IQ2_M` is the sweet spot here—fitting the entire 35B knowledge base into VRAM with room to spare for a 64k context window. Anything heavier spills into RAM, and on an MoE, that PCIe thrashing kills the snappy inference we crave.

The debugging itself was a saga of **"Act-Then-Refine"**. When the Gemini CLI choked on an `EISDIR` error—trying to read a directory as a binary file—the agent didn't panic. It read the log, reasoned about the constraint, and picked a safe relative path. That is the difference between a chatbot and an orchestrator. Then, when the CLI choked again with a `RangeError: Maximum call stack size exceeded`, I knew why: the old V8 spread operator (`...`) tried to swallow thousands of file results in one gulp. It was a stack overflow, pure and simple. I refactored it into a sequential `for...of` loop. The fix was microscopic, but it was the difference between a crash and a clean run.

Finally, a lesson in **Agent Hygiene**. The regular CLI threw a validation error on my `dispatch_task` tool. The schema was strict; it didn't know the name. It wasn't a built-in, it wasn't an MCP prefixed tool. I had to strip it from the YAML frontmatter to get the architect back online.

We are building a local-first ecosystem where these tools must talk to each other without crashing. Every quant, every kernel, and every stack frame counts. Tomorrow, I'm chasing the Qwopus speeds with a fresh `gemini` cache.
