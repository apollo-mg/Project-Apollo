# Dev Diary: 2026-01-07

## The Mechanical Ghost in the Machine

Today was a lesson in the violent friction between mathematical perfection and physical reality. I spent the afternoon wrestling with the resonance of my 3D printer—specifically, the Y-axis, which is currently acting like a tuning fork for every vibration the toolhead generates. 

I tried a desperate, "mad scientist" hack: wrapping wire around the hotend shaft and tensioning it with pliers to add rigidity to the toolhead. It was a crude way to fight the flex, but the results in the resonance tests were eye-opening.

The X-axis responded beautifully. The wire brace stiffened the connection so much that the main resonance peak shifted up to 165 Hz. It turned the X-axis into a stiff, reliable rail. But the Y-axis? The Y-axis is a stubborn bastard. The wire added a bit of mass and shifted the frequency, but it didn't stop the "double hump" in the spectrum—the axis is still twisting and flexing in a way that no amount of wire can fix.

This is where the "Soul" of the project—the desire for a high-speed, high-precision machine—hits the "System" of the silicon and the physical laws of motion.

I sat down with Gemini to parse the data. We had to decide between raw speed and surface quality. The automated suggestions for the shaper (the algorithm that cancels out vibration) were chasing high acceleration numbers, but the residual vibration was too high—around 10%. That's the difference between a clean print and a print that looks like it was etched by a shaky hand.

We made a pivot. We abandoned the pursuit of "maximum acceleration" for "maximum stability."

I opted for the `ei` (Exponential Input) shaper. It’s a more robust, "brute force" approach to smoothing. It effectively kills the vibration peaks, but at a cost: it caps my acceleration. We settled on a global limit of 9,000 mm/s². It’s slower than the theoretical limit of the motors, but it’s a massive leap over a standard build. We are basically building a machine that has the soul of a racer but the smooth gait of a luxury sedan.

Then came the real kicker: Square Corner Velocity (SCV). 

I realized that the "violence" of the motion isn't just about how fast the motors spin, but how they change direction. The SCV dictates how much "jerk" happens at a 90-degree turn. If I tell the machine to snap from moving East to moving North instantly, it's essentially a hammer blow to the frame. Given the flex in my printed toolhead, a high SCV is a death sentence for quality. 

I've decided to drop the SCV to 5. It’s a compromise—the corners will be microscopically rounded—but it will stop the "shockwave" that causes the Y-axis to ring.

**Current State of the Build:**
- **X-Axis:** `2hump_ei` @ 149.8 Hz (Ultra-stiff, zero vibration).
- **Y-Axis:** `ei` @ 72.0 Hz (Robust smoothing, 9.5k accel cap).
- **Global Acceleration:** 9,000 mm/s².
- **SCV:** 5 (Smoothing the directional snap).

It’s not the 20k acceleration I dreamed of, but it’s a machine that actually *works* without looking like a vibrating mess. The local-first philosophy isn't just about the AI; it's about the hardware being as predictable and controllable as the code.

Next step: See if this stability holds up under actual print loads, or if the Y-axis flex eventually breaks the simulation.
