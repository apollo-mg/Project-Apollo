# December 3, 2025: The RDNA 4 War Room

The air in the room is thick with the smell of ozone and overclocked silicon. I’m staring at a terminal that refuses to tell me the truth. 

We’ve hit the wall. The "Soul" of Project Apollo—this incredible, bleeding-edge video generation capability with Wan 2.1—is being strangled by the "System." Specifically, the brutal, uncompromising math kernels of the RDNA 4 architecture.

### The Mismatch: Half vs. Float

It started with a classic, violent `RuntimeError`. 

`expected scalar type Half but found Float`.

On paper, it looks like a simple typing error. In reality, it’s a clash of civilizations. We’re running the Wan 2.1 model in FP16/BF16, but the T5 Text Encoder is spitting out FP32 embeddings. When those two worlds collide inside the attention mechanism, the ROCm drivers don't just "fix it"—they scream. The `einsum` operations in the `attention_split` module were hitting a wall because the tensors weren't aligned. RDNA 4 is a beast, but it’s a beast that demands perfect memory contiguity and absolute type consistency. Anything less, and the whole pipeline collapses into a heap of segmentation faults.

I spent the last few hours in the guts of `comfy/ldm/modules/attention.py`. I had to force the system to play nice. I implemented a patch to ensure that the `q`, `k`, and `v` tensors are explicitly cast to match the query's dtype and, more importantly, forced them to be `contiguous()` in memory. It’s a heavy-handed fix, but on this hardware, "heavy-handed" is the only way to survive.

### The Ghost in the Machine

Then came the "Hang."

The system didn't just crash; it went silent. The UI was unresponsive, the terminal was a void, and the logs... the logs were a nightmare. I was chasing a phantom. I thought I was dealing with a deadlocked process, but it was actually a mess of unbuffered output and broken symlinks. The `tee` command in the startup script was swallowing errors, and the `python -u` flag was making the logs feel like they were written in disappearing ink.

There’s a specific kind of madness that sets in when you're debugging a zombie process that's still technically running but functionally dead. I had to manually kill the PID, fight with the virtual environment activation, and essentially perform a digital exorcism on the server just to get a clean slate.

### The Optimization Trap

The temptation to squeeze out more performance is constant. We talked about `TunableOp` and `SDMA`. 

`TunableOp` is the "Crash Maker." It’s designed to find the fastest kernel, but on the gfx1201 architecture, those "fast" kernels are often unstable, leading to immediate "Illegal Memory Access" errors. It’s a high-stakes gamble. 

`SDMA` is the "Corruptor." It’s great for moving massive chunks of data from RAM to VRAM, but if it slips up, you don't get a crash—you get "silent corruption." You get a beautiful video that looks like a psychedelic fever dream because a few bits flipped during a DMA transfer.

For now, we are choosing stability over raw speed. We're sticking with `split-cross-attention`. It’s slower, but it’s predictable.

### The Lesson

The takeaway from today is clear: Local-first AI on AMD isn't about finding the fastest path; it's about finding the *survivable* path. We are building an ecosystem where the architecture has to be as much about memory management and type alignment as it is about neural weights. 

The next step is to refine the fallback mechanism. If Flash Attention fails, I don't want the system to just stumble into a generic SDPA error. I want it to intelligently pivot to our patched `split-cross-attention`. 

We’re close. The machine is temperamental, but we’re finally starting to speak its language.
