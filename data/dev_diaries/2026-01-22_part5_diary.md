# Project Apollo Dev Diary: 2026-01-22 (Part 5)

## The Ghost in the Machine: The Eddy Saga

The struggle between the "Soul" of the machine—its pure, high-speed potential—and the "System"—the fickle, temperature-sensitive reality of the silicon—has reached a fever pitch. Today was a brutal cycle of calibration, failure, and architectural pivoting.

### The Bandwidth Bottleneck
Early in the session, I was fighting a stuttering toolhead. I suspected the Klipper/Raspberry Pi combo was choking on the USB bandwidth coming from the RP2040 on the Eddy sensor. The LDC1612 chip is a beast, pumping data faster than the Python-based Klipper host could ingest. 

I adjusted the `sample_period` to 0.000500 and disabled the software mesh interpolation (`mesh_pps: 0, 0`). It felt like tuning a radio frequency; we were smoothing out the data stream to prevent buffer overruns. It worked. The rapid scan finally kicked in, and seeing the bed sweep with that level of precision felt like seeing the future.

### The Legacy Crups
I found a piece of "ghost code" in the `G28` macro. It was a legacy wrapper from the beta days, designed to force a probe after homing to fix temperature drift. In the new architecture, it was just noise—a redundant step that would have caused double-probing and Z-offset hell. I purged it. Clean code is the only way to maintain a system this complex.

### The Kinematic Geometry
The Z-Tilt adjustment is the most intellectually taxing part of the hardware. Because the bed is a kinematic mount—resting on spherical bearings—the "pivot points" are not the bed corners, but the actual lead screw locations. 

I realized my configuration was fundamentally skewed. The X-axis symmetry was broken, which meant the Z-Tilt algorithm was "chasing its tail," trying to correct a tilt that the geometry couldn't actually support. I measured the actual physical coordinates of the lead screws: 
`FL: -75, -3`
`FR: 335, -3`
`R: 155, 375`

The "triangle" of the Z-Tilt is far more twisted than the software initially thought. Once I updated the `z_positions` to these exact coordinates, the convergence improved significantly. The algorithm now understands the real mechanical leverage.

### The Temperature Wall
Then came the nightmare: The Temperature Compensation. 

I ran the Eddy temperature calibration while the chamber was hot. It seemed successful. Then, the system crashed. `Invalid probe_eddy_current height`. 

This is the classic friction between the physics of the LDC1612 sensor and the digital model. The sensor's resonant frequency shifts wildly with temperature. When I tried to home, the sensor was reading a "frequency" that mapped to a distance far outside the calibration curve, causing the math to collapse into a `NaN` error.

The sensor is hyper-sensitive. If the calibration curve is generated while the machine is at 60°C, but the homing occurs when the sensor is still warming up or cooling down, the "drift" is too aggressive for the software to handle without a massive, high-fidelity `[temperature_probe]` map.

**The current strategy:** I have to perform a "Synchronized Heat Soak." I need to get the entire system—sensor, bed, and chamber—to a stable, high temperature, then perform the Drive Current calibration AND the Temperature Profile calibration in one single, high-heat window. If the sensor is still erratic after that, it's not a configuration issue; it's a defective LDC1612 chip.

### Current Status
- **Hardware:** Bed-mesh range restored after cutting lead screws; Z-travel increased.
- **Software:** Z-Tilt geometry corrected; `MOTORS_OFF` macro implemented for silence.
- **Next Step:** The "Hot Soak" calibration gauntlet. 

This machine is no longer a standard 3D printer. It's a high-precision instrument, and like all instruments, it refuses to make things easy for me.
