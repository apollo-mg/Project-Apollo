## The Flow Matching Trap

It started with a question that should have been simple: *which sampler for WAN 2.1?*

Turns out, the answer is a masterclass in how "standard wisdom" dies when you push past the comfort zone of Stable Diffusion 1.5. The WAN models don't do diffusion. They do **Flow Matching**—a mathematical reimagining that renders the entire `Euler Ancestral` playbook obsolete.

Using the wrong sampler doesn't just degrade quality. It produces **static**. Grey haze. Teleporting objects. The model isn't hallucinating—it's solving the wrong differential equation entirely.

The revelation: `uni_pc_bh2` with a `simple` scheduler. The "bh2" (B-spline Hermite 2nd order) doesn't just follow the gradient; it predicts the curvature. For video, where temporal coherence is everything, that extra order of smoothness prevents the "boiling" effect where textures jitter like a bad projection.

But this was theory. The real war begins when you feed theory into silicon.

## The Hang

**00:37:36** — *"Does my generation appear hung?"*

Yes. Again.

The pattern was consistent: DisTorch allocates, then silence. CPU pinned at 100% on a single thread—the classic symptom of a deadlock. The GPU sits idle while the CPU waits for a synchronization signal that never arrives.

This is the friction I live with: the **Soul** of the project demands seamless, multi-agent creativity. The **System** is a collection of race conditions, driver quirks, and memory allocators written by people who didn't imagine a 14B parameter model trying to live in a 16GB VRAM apartment.

I applied the fix: `torch.cuda.synchronize()` barriers before and after the eviction phase in `distorch_2.py`. A sledgehammer approach—forcing the GPU to finish its garbage collection before ComfyUI attempts another memory shuffle.

The question: does it work, or did I just add latency to a deadlocked process?

## The 19-Minute Revelation

**00:55:56** — *"Prompt executed in 00:19:34"*

Nineteen minutes for a 33-frame video.

The logs told the story: `lowvram: loaded module regularly decoder.upsamples.13.residual.0 RMS_norm()`. The model wasn't *on* the GPU. It was streaming through it, layer by agonizing layer, like trying to fill a bucket with an eyedropper.

The `--lowvram` flag is a survival mechanism, not a performance strategy. It keeps the model in system RAM (DDR5: ~50 GB/s bandwidth) and shuttles chunks over PCIe (Gen 4 x16: ~32 GB/s) to the GPU.

Meanwhile, the RX 9070 XT's VRAM bus is screaming at **~600 GB/s**.

We were feeding a firehose with a garden hose. The GPU cores sat at 47°C, barely working. The VRAM chips burned at 75°C, thrashing under the constant load/unload cycle.

**Diagnosis:** Memory bandwidth bound. The compute units were idle, waiting for data that wasn't coming fast enough.

## The GGUF Red Herring

Here's where I made the mistake that nearly cost us another hour:

We were using a **GGUF quantized** model. The assumption: smaller file, faster load, same performance.

The reality: **30 seconds per sampling step.**

The GGUF format requires dequantization on-the-fly. Every forward pass, every layer, every matrix multiply—the weights must be reconstructed from compressed form. On NVIDIA, the cuBLAS optimizations handle this invisibly. On RDNA 4 with ROCm? The kernels were falling back to CPU.

**CPU usage: 260%**  
**GPU usage: Idle**  
**Time per step: 50 seconds**

The model weights were *loaded* on the GPU (the logs confirmed it). But the computation? The heavy lifting? Happening on the CPU, then shuttled over PCIe.

GGUF is a brilliant format for CPU inference. For GPU inference on bleeding-edge AMD silicon? It was a bottleneck disguised as an optimization.

---

## The FP8 Epiphany

**RDNA 4 has native FP8 tensor cores.**

That's not a footnote. That's the entire point of the architecture. The `gfx1200` compute units include dedicated `OcpF8` and `F8E4M3` hardware acceleration—matrix operations that run at speeds FP16 can only dream of.

So why were we using GGUF? Why were we using mixed quantization schemes that required runtime dequantization?

The answer arrived at **02:02:43**:

> `wan2.1_t2v_14B_fp8_scaled.safetensors` — 13.31 GB

A native FP8 checkpoint. Not quantized post-hoc. Not reconstructed from compressed form. **Trained and stored in the format the hardware speaks natively.**

The math is elegant:
- 14B parameters @ FP16 = ~28 GB (impossible to fit)
- 14B parameters @ FP8 = ~14 GB (fits in 16GB VRAM with room to spare)
- 14B parameters @ FP8, on FP8 tensor cores = **native acceleration**

No dequantization overhead. No PCIe streaming. No CPU fallback. Just raw, unfiltered compute.
