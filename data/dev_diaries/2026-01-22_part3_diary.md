# Apollo Dev Diary: The Eddy Struggle

**2026-01-22**

Today was a brutal lesson in the difference between "theoretical stability" and the messy, entropic reality of hardware. I set out to implement native adaptive meshing on the Apollo, moving away from KAMP macros to the pure, mainline Klipper implementation. It seemed like a clean, architectural move—stripping the bloat to let the hardware breathe. Instead, I spent the next several hours wrestling with a sensor that feels more like a temperamental ghost than a piece of precision silicon.

### The Friction of the Swap
The transition from KAMP to mainline Klipper was supposed to be a simple `sed` command. But as any developer knows, automation without nuance is just a faster way to break things. My aggressive deletion of the KAMP macros left orphaned `gcode:` blocks in my config, causing Klipper to throw a fit about the `screws_tilt_adjust` section. I managed to clean up the wreckage, but the victory was hollow.

When I tried to run the native `BED_MESH_CALIBRATE METHOD=scan`, the system balked. It wouldn't perform the sweep; it just Z-hopped like a primitive probe. I thought it was a parameter mismatch—`rapid_scan` vs `scan`. I thought it was a density issue. I thought it was a safety check on the overshoot. I was chasing ghosts.

### The Crash
Then, the hardware struck back. After a painstaking calibration of the Z-offset, the Z-axis decided to ignore my commands and drive straight into the bed. I heard the mechanical crunch of the plastic mount breaking. It’s a bitter feeling—knowing your software logic is responsible for the physical destruction of your machine.

The post-mortem was even more frustrating. The "Invalid probe_eddy_current height" error is a nightmare of abstraction. Klipper's Eddy implementation is incredibly sensitive to the induction curve. If the frequency reading drifts even slightly outside the calibrated range—due to temperature or a tiny shift in the probe's mounting height—the whole system rejects the measurement. 

I tried everything:
*   Re-calibrated the drive current (`reg_drive_current`) from 14 to 18.
*   Re-ran the induction mapping multiple times.
*   Adjusted the Z-offset manually.

Nothing worked. The sensor is reporting "Invalid Height" the moment it tries to home, because the "Open Air" reading (the frequency when the sensor is far from the bed) is falling outside the mathematical curve I just spent an hour building. It's a circular trap: the calibration is too narrow to handle the distance, and the distance is too great for the calibration to handle.

### Lessons from the Silicon
The takeaway for the Apollo ecosystem is clear: Local-first AI and high-precision hardware require a different kind of "calibration." You can't just treat a sensor like a switch; it's a continuous-variable observer. The friction between the **Soul** (my goal of a seamless, high-speed, adaptive-mesh printer) and the **System** (the extreme sensitivity of the LDC1612 chip) is immense.

I'm currently sitting with a broken mount and a sensor that refuses to acknowledge the concept of "distance." The architecture is sound, but the hardware implementation is currently too volatile for the software's current constraints. 

**Next Step:** I need to investigate the raw frequency output of the Eddy. If we can find out exactly what frequency is being reported in "Open Air," we can decide if we need to expand the calibration range or if the hardware itself is fundamentally misaligned with the nozzle.

**Current Status:** Hardware broken. Software logic in a loop. Need coffee.
