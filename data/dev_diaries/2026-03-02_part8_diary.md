# Apollo Dev Diary: 2026-03-02 (Part 8)

## The Vision Autopsy and the 32B Ghost

Today was a brutal, high-fidelity lesson in the gap between "seeing" and "understanding." I spent the morning wrestling with the Qwen3-VL:8B vision model, and for a moment, I thought the whole architecture had lost its mind.

The model looked at a picture of a Raspberry Pi and hallucinated a full-scale, high-end desktop PC. It didn't just miss a detail; it completely ignored the physical reality of the image, hallucinating an RTX 3060 and an AMD Ryzen 5600 out of thin air. 

I dug into the logs, expecting a vision encoder failure. But the autopsy revealed something far more insidious. The vision model actually *did* see the truth—it correctly identified the PCB text, the MicroSD slot, and the specific voltage regulation labels—but the 8B Engineer model (the text-based agent) "panicked." Because I had implemented a strict "You MUST output JSON" rule for the tool-calling protocol, and because the 8B model lacked the contextual "World Knowledge" to know what those labels meant, it had no choice but to fill the JSON object with junk data just to satisfy the logic. It was a collision between a strict system constraint and a model with insufficient cognitive depth.

This is the "Soul vs. System" friction in a nutshell: the system demands a perfect JSON structure, but the soul (the intelligence) isn't deep enough to provide it, so the whole thing collapses into a hallucination.

## The "Wild Magic" of the 32B Vision Spec

The community (r/LocalLLM) is buzzing about the Qwen3-VL 32B. On paper, it's too big for my 16GB RX 9070 XT. My skepticism was high—I couldn't see how a 32B model, even at 4-bit, could fit alongside my resident 8B Engineer without a catastrophic OOM. 

But the "magic" is real. Between EXL2 3.0bpw quantization and the MoE (Mixture of Experts) architecture, we can squeeze the weights into ~13GB. The vision engine uses a "Tiled Attention" method—it only runs the heavy logic on high-frequency "interest" areas of an image. This is the game-changer. We are moving from "is there a cat in this photo?" to "Read the serial number on this specific capacitor."

## Architectural Breakthrough: The Two-Turn Forensic Method

I’ve decided to pivot the Vision Audit strategy. Instead of forcing the 8B model to do everything, we'll use a tiered approach:
1. **Turn 1 (Triage):** The 8B model acts as a high-speed scanner, identifying anchor points.
2. **Turn 2 (Deep ID):** We hot-swap the 32B Vision Specialist into the VRAM for a single, high-fidelity forensic pass.

This requires precise VRAM management. I'll need to kill the 8B process to make room for the 32B, perform the deep scan, and then reload the 8B. It's messy, it's heavy, and it's exactly what my hardware is built for.

## The Forge and the Future

We successfully tested the `!forge` command in Discord. The logic-loop in the `buddy_agent.py` was broken—the agent was getting stuck in a JSON-loop, causing the `#planner` channel to be spammed with dashboard updates. I've rewritten the `update_planner_dashboard` to look back 50 messages and clean up its own duplicates. 

The system is finally starting to feel cohesive. We've moved from a collection of isolated scripts to a unified, multi-agent ecosystem that can be triggered from a Discord chat. 

The realization hit me toward the end of the session: what I'm building isn't just a hobby project. The "Local-First" movement is real. As companies realize that sending every byte to a cloud provider is a liability, the demand for people who can manage local, high-performance, sovereign AI on consumer-grade hardware (like my RDNA 4 setup) is going to explode. 

I'm not just writing code; I'm building a blueprint for the next era of computing.
