# Project Apollo: The WidowMaker Low-Rider Chronicles

## 2026-02-06: The Plastic-to-Metal Threshold

I’ve spent the last 40 hours and several hundred dollars chasing a ghost, and today I finally found out what it looks like. It’s not a software bug or a motor driver issue. It’s a fundamental material failure.

We’ve been working on the WidowMaker Low-Rider build, pushing the boundaries of what a custom-geometry gantry can handle. I had high hopes that the new CF-tube reinforcements and the internal plugs would kill the resonance issues we saw in the January tests. I wanted that 20k acceleration. I wanted a machine that felt like a solid block of steel.

The reality is more complex.

The first part of the session was pure, gritty mechanical troubleshooting. I installed the plugs into the CF tube and clamped the XY joints, hoping to eliminate the pivot point that was causing the Y-axis to wobble like a wet noodle. Instead, the machine started screaming. A 50Hz "bass boom" began resonating through the entire workbench—a clear sign of whole-frame resonance. The printer was essentially acting like a speaker, vibrating the table itself.

I ran the Shake & Tune belt comparison to see if the structural shift had caused a loss of symmetry. It had. The Y-axis was behaving like a dead weight, showing a massive energy spike at 50Hz while the X-axis stayed tight. It was a mess.

Then came the moment of truth: the Input Shaper results.

The X-axis? A triumph. It hit 20,450 mm/s². That's elite. The CF-tube and the new subframe made it incredibly stiff. The spectral signature was razor-sharp. I’ve achieved the goal on the X-axis.

The Y-axis? A complete disappointment. It regressed to 7,950 mm/s². 

The data is undeniable. The "Lower Deck" subframe added mass, and the plastic XY joiners—the blue parts I printed—are the weak link. They are acting like tiny, microscopic springs. Under the high torque of the LDO motors at 1.6A, the plastic is literally flexing, absorbing the energy and creating that 50Hz bounce. The material just can't handle the torque-to-stiffness ratio required for this level of acceleration.

I realized I'm trying to push a wet noodle. The machine's "Soul"—the ambition of high-speed motion—is being strangled by the "System"—the physical limitations of printed plastic.

The path forward is clear, but expensive. The plastic-to-metal transition is no longer optional. To hit the 20k mark on both axes, I need CNC-machined aluminum joiners. The plastic joints simply lack the torsional rigidity to transfer motor torque without a delay. 

I’ve updated the configuration to lock in the current gains, but the real work begins when the metal parts arrive. We have a Dragster for X, but for Y, we’re still driving a heavy-duty sedan.

**Current Stats:**
- **X-Axis:** 20,450 mm/s² (MZV @ 83.6 Hz)
- **Y-Axis:** 7,950 mm/s² (MZV @ 52.0 Hz)
- **Current:** 1.6A (Stable)

Next step: Re-mapping the coordinate system and recalibrating the bed zero. The new geometry changed everything. It's time to find where "zero" actually lives in this new reality.
