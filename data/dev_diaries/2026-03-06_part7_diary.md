# Dev Diary: The VRAM Wall and the GGUF Betrayal

**Date:** 2026-03-06
**Location:** Localhost / Tailscale Remote
**Hardware:** AMD RX 9070 XT (RDNA 4)

### The VRAM Wall

It started with a classic architectural mismatch. I was trying to force `qwen35` through the `rocm/vllm-dev:rocm7.2_navi` image, and the system slapped me with a `ValueError`. The bridge wasn't built yet.

So, I pivoted. I threw `neuralmagic/Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct-FP8` into the ring as a baseline. It worked, but at a terrible cost. vLLM, in its infinite wisdom, aggressively pre-allocates **90% of your VRAM** for the KV cache the moment it spins up, regardless of model size. My 7B model sat there, sucking down **16.25 GB of 16 GB** of my precious VRAM, leaving absolutely zero headroom for anything else. It was a memory hog that choked the ecosystem before it even took a breath.

I had to kill it. The "Soul" of the project—the multi-agent ecosystem—needed breathing room.

### The Two-Pronged Sandbox Strategy

I needed `Qwen 3.5`, but the hardware was screaming. I engaged Gemini to build a **Two-Pronged Sandbox Strategy**:

1.  **The Fast Path:** Pull `rocm/vllm-dev:nightly`. This is the bleeding edge. High risk, high reward. It might have the Qwen 3.5 kernels, or it might be a house of cards.
2.  **The Sure Path:** Build a custom sandbox (`apollo/vllm-qwen35:sandbox`). I’d inject the latest vLLM wheels into the known-good Navi image.

While the Docker daemon was busy decompressing massive layers (the I/O wait spiked to 32%, the NVMe was screaming), I realized something crucial about my workflow. I’m not just a coder anymore; I’m a remote operator. I set up Tailscale SSH and `tmux` to monitor this from my phone. The terminal is no longer just on my desk; it’s in my pocket.

### The GGUF Betrayal

Here is where the friction hit hard. I had a shiny new `Qwen3.5-9b-heretic-v2-q8_0.gguf` model. I fed it to the sandbox.

**It failed.**

`ImportError`. `Architecture mismatch`.

The realization was bitter but necessary: **GGUF is for inference engines like Ollama or Llama.cpp.** It’s a portable format, lightweight and compatible. But vLLM is a heavyweights engine built for PyTorch. It demands the raw, uncut **Safetensors** format to unlock its custom CUDA/HIP kernels. Trying to run GGUF in vLLM was like trying to fit a square peg into a round hole made of silicon. The "System" rejected the "Soul" because the wrapper was wrong.

### The Nightly Breakthrough

I switched tactics. I pointed the nightly build toward the **Qwen 3.5 9B Safetensors** model.

And then, the logs told the story of a victory:
*   `Resolved architecture: Qwen3_5ForConditionalGeneration`
*   `Capturing CUDA graphs...`
*   `API server listening at http://0.0.0.0:8000`

It worked. The `nightly` image (v0.16.1) finally bridged the gap. The 9B model loaded, the weights settled at 12.5 GB (a massive improvement over the 7B's greedy hogging), and the API came alive.

### Core Lessons for the Apollo Ecosystem

1.  **VRAM is a Tyrant:** We cannot rely on default vLLM settings. We need a `gpu_memory_utilization` flag in our launcher scripts that respects the sub-24GB reality of consumer cards like the 9070 XT. If we don't cap it, the model will eat the system.
2.  **Format Discipline:** The multi-agent system must standardize on **Safetensors** for the vLLM agents. GGUF is too fragile for this layer of the stack. We need a conversion pipeline to ensure every agent pulls from the same, optimized format.
3.  **The "Nightly" Gamble:** We cannot wait for stable releases. The bridge between RDNA 4 hardware and new architectures is built on nightly builds. Our deployment pipeline must be able to spin up "sandbox" containers dynamically, test the new architecture, and then lock it in if it works.

The API is live. The phone is connected. The sandbox is breathing. We are one step closer to a fully local-first, multi-agent mind.
