# Project Apollo: The Tensor Dimension War

December 3, 2025

Everything is running on the bleeding edge, which is exactly why everything is currently broken.

I’m sitting here in the glow of the RX 9070 XT, staring at a screen filled with terminal-induced madness. My goal was simple: get the Wan2.1 video generation model running locally with GGUF quantization—the holy grail of high-fidelity video on a

desktop-grade GPU. I’m trying to build a multi-agent system where the "Soul" (the creative logic) is driving the "System" (the silicon-level weights), but right now, the system is just screaming at me in `RuntimeError`.

### The Crash: A Dimensional Mismatch

The error is a classic, a beautiful, infuriating mismatch. 

`RuntimeError: The size of tensor a (5120) must match the size of tensor b (3520) at non-singleton dimension 1`

It happened deep in the `KSamplerAdvanced` node during a `UniPC` sampling loop. I was watching the logs—the GGUF blocks moving onto the GPU, the `Moving blocks.39.self_attn.q.weight to GPU` ritual that feels like a digital invocation—and then, *boom*. 

The trace points directly to the `ComfyUI-GGUF` custom node. It’s happening during a weight casting operation. Specifically, in `forward_ggml_cast_weights`. The system is trying to cast a weight tensor from a quantized state back to a usable format for the attention mechanism, and the math just... stops making sense. 

Dimension 5120 versus 3520. One is the expected shape of the weight block, the other is the actual shape being fed into the operation. It’s a fundamental architectural collision. It looks like a mismatch between the model’s internal configuration and how the GGUF quantizer is slicing up the weight matrices.

### The Friction: Soul vs. Silicon

This is where the "Soul" of Project Apollo hits the wall of the "System." 

The "Soul" wants to generate high-fidelity video, to move fluidly from text to motion. But the "System" is bound by the cold, hard limits of quantization. We are trying to squeeze a massive model into 16GB of VRAM. The `lowvram` flags and `reserve-vram 3.0` settings are working hard, but they are creating a fragmented memory landscape where the shapes of the tensors are getting lost in translation between the GGUF implementation and the ROCm-driven backend.

It’s the tension of the local-first movement. We don't want to rely on a massive server farm in the cloud; we want this power on my desk. But to get that power, we have to play with the very edges of mathematical stability.

### Technical Epiphanies and the Path Forward

The logs show the `GGUF_GPU_RESIDENT` logic is doing its job, moving blocks of the model into VRAM with surgical precision. But the error is happening at the exact moment the attention mechanism tries to look at those weights. 

It’s not just a memory error; it’s a structural error. 

**The Lessons Learned:**
1. **Quantization is a double-edged sword.** GGUF is the only way to fit Wan2.1 on a 9070 XT, but the way it handles the dimension mapping in the `self_attn` blocks is clearly prone to breakage when the model architecture deviates from standard transformer shapes.
2. **ROCm is powerful but unforgiving.** The way PyTorch is interacting with the ROCm 7.1.1 driver during the weight-casting phase is clearly hitting a bottleneck where the tensor shapes are being miscalculated.
3. **The "Multi-Agent" vision needs a stable substrate.** If I want my agents to actually *do* things—to direct video generation—they need a predictable, stable math engine. A dimension mismatch is a death sentence for a creative agent.

### Next Steps: The Reconnaissance

I need to go back into the guts of the `ComfyUI-GGUF` op-code. This isn't a simple "change a setting" fix. This is a "fix the way we slice the weights" fix. I need to see if the `cast_bias_weight` function is misinterpreting the dimensions of the weight blocks during the `forward_ggml_cast_weights` call.

Is it a mismatch in the model's config file? Or is the GGUF implementation assuming a different head dimension than what Wan2.1 actually uses?

Tomorrow, we dive into the tensor shapes. We're not just building a model; we're building a way to survive the silicon.
