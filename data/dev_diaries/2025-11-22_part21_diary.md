## Part 21 — Memory Fragmentation, Model Bias, and the Yellow Triangle of Death

### The Hang That Wouldn't Die

We're fighting ghosts in the memory allocator again.

The DisTorch multi-GPU hang on subsequent generations wasn't a bug—it was physics. The RDNA 4 driver wasn't flushing its operation queue before we tried to reclaim VRAM. We were deallocating pointers while CUDA kernels were still breathing on the metal.

The fix was surgical, almost embarrassing in its simplicity:

```python
torch.cuda.synchronize()  # Make the silicon wait for itself
gc.collect()  # Force Python to let go
mm.soft_empty_cache()  # Tell ROCm to release its grip
```

Three lines. But those lines represent something deeper: **the gap between what our code thinks it owns and what the hardware actually holds**.

The user's 16GB RX 9070 XT has Wan 2.2 consuming 8.88GB. That leaves 7GB "free"—except it doesn't. It leaves 7GB of fragmented, pinned, partially-allocated space that the driver refuses to release until we *synchronize* and *beg*.

`reserve-vram=1.0` wasn't about saving memory. It was about leaving the driver enough slack that it wouldn't choke on its own bookkeeping.

### Soul vs. Alignment

A question that cut through the technical noise:

> *"WAN 2.2 doesn't seem to do nude body parts very well at all."*

This is where the creative ambitions of this project collide with the corporate fingerprints baked into these models. Wan 2.2 isn't *filtering*—it's **unaware**. The dataset was scrubbed before training. The concept doesn't exist in its latent space the way we expect it to.

Here's the epiphany:

**Text-to-Video:** Wan looks up "nude" in its internal dictionary, finds a vague, RLHF-sanitized concept, and generates "Barbie doll" anatomy because that's what it knows.

**Image-to-Video:** Feed Wan a Flux-generated frame with perfect anatomy, and something interesting happens. Wan stops trying to *imagine* and starts trying to *move*. It's encoding latents, applying physics to curves and textures. It doesn't need to understand what it's animating—just that *these pixels* move *that way*.

But there's a barrier: **conceptual rejection**. Even without an active safety filter, heavy RLHF tuning can cause "clothing hallucination"—the model sees input pixels and tries to "fix" them, dressing the anatomy because its internal bias says *people wear clothes*.

The "Pro Workflow" emerging for this ecosystem:
1. **Flux** generates the keyframe (prompt adherence, lighting, anatomy—top tier)
2. **Wan 2.1 I2V** animates it (native motion physics, temporal consistency)

We're not choosing a model. We're **orchestrating a pipeline** where each model does what it understands.

### The Yellow Triangle of Death

I should have known the real battle wouldn't be in the GPU stack. It would be in the Linux kernel's handling of a $150 MSI motherboard.

**MSI B550-A PRO.** Nuvoton NCT6687D Super I/O chip. The chip that refuses to play nice with mainline Linux.

The `nct6683` driver loaded. It detected the fans. It showed RPM readings:
- fan2: 1428 RPM
- fan3: 1246 RPM  
- fan4: 1826 RPM

But CoolerControl displayed the yellow triangle: *"The currently installed driver does not support control of this channel."*

Read-only. The kernel had claimed the device but refused to write to the PWM registers. Safety. Conservatism. A thousand years of hardware abstraction paying rent in lost control.

`force=1` didn't work. The `nct6775` fallback rejected the device entirely.

The solution was a DKMS module from a GitHub repo that didn't even have the correct URL in my notes (`Fred777` → `Fred78290`). The `nct6687d` driver—community-maintained, kernel-bypassing, the kind of thing Windows users get for free with a BIOS update.

After installation:
```
nct6687d-1.0: installed
Module loaded successfully
CoolerControl daemon restarted
```

The yellow triangle vanished.

**This is the reality of local-first AI infrastructure:** You're not just deploying models. You're wrestling with kernel modules, driver permissions, sensor virtualization, and the gap between what "open source" promises and what actually ships.

### The Max Sensor

The final piece: a virtual sensor that takes the maximum temperature across CPU package, GPU edge, and GPU VRAM.

Why VRAM? Because that's where the heat lives during inference. The GPU core might idle at 47°C while the memory runs at 70°C, churning through 12GB of weights per second.

The curve:
- 40°C → 30% (silent idle)
- 60°C → 50% (audible, acceptable)
- 75°C → 80% (heavy load)
- 85°C → 100% (emergency)

This isn't overclocking. This is **thermal budgeting** for a system that will be asked to hold 20GB of models in 16GB of VRAM and pretend it's normal.

### What I'm Carrying Forward

1. **Synchronization is not optional.** The RDNA 4 driver requires explicit barriers. `torch.cuda.synchronize()` isn't a suggestion—it's a contract with the hardware.

2. **Native vs. Adapted matters.** Wan understands video. Flux understands images. Combining them (Flux keyframe → Wan I2V) beats forcing either model to do what it wasn't trained to do.

3. **Linux hardware control is not solved.** The gap between Windows and Linux for fan control, sensor access, and motherboard-level features is not closing. It's a permanent cost of running local.

4. **The "Soul" of this system lives in the gaps.** Between model and model, between driver and kernel, between what the silicon promises and what it delivers—that's where the actual work happens.
