# Project Apollo: Dev Diary - 2026-03-05 (Part 9)

The fight for local-first sovereignty is rarely about high-level logic; it is almost always a brutal, low-level war against the silicon. Today was a masterclass in the friction between the "Soul"—the dream of running 80B parameter models on a consumer desktop—and the "System"—the rigid, often broken, and poorly documented realities of the ROCm 7.2.0 driver stack.

## The Battle of the ABI and the Wavefront
The day began with the realization that the "Official Documentation" is a lie. It is a dictionary of what *should* exist, not a manual of what *actually* breaks. We hit the wall of the **HipBLASLT ABI Drift**. The ROCm 7.2.0 headers had renamed `hipblasDatatype_t` to `hipDataType`, but the PyTorch source was still clinging to the old name. 

It took surgical precision to patch it, but the deeper realization came when we discussed the architecture. Your RX 9070 XT isn't an enterprise chip. It lives in the **Wave32** world—built for the agility of gaming and irregular workloads. The enterprise MI300X lives in **Wave64**, built for pure-math throughput. This isn't just a technical distinction; it's the fundamental reason why we had to hack the `C10_WARP_SIZE` constants. We had to force the software to acknowledge the physical reality of your RDNA 4 compute units.

## The C/C++ Extension Trap
Then came the "Code Writers" landmines. The build system tried to apply C++17 standards to files still labeled with a `.c` extension. The compiler, being a pedantic bastard, refused to compile.

We had to perform a series of rapid-fire "Combat Repairs":
*   **The File Rename:** We had to hunt down `cpython_defs.c`, `eval_frame.c`, and `dim_opcode.c` and force them into the `.cpp` world.
*   **The Macro Fix:** We patched `extra_state.h` because a `void*` couldn't play nice with the stricter C++ type-checking for `ExtraState*`.
*   **The Cache Flush:** Every time we renamed a file, we had to purge the CMake cache to ensure the build system didn't try to use old, stale object files.

It was a grinding, repetitive cycle of: *Compile -> Crash -> Rename -> Re-compile*.

## The Victory: A Lean, Mean Engine
After hours of 100% CPU saturation, the smoke cleared. We didn't just get a generic wheel; we got a **sovereign** one. 

The final `.whl` file is only **352 MB**. Compare that to the 2GB+ monster you get from a standard PyTorch install. By compiling specifically for **GFX1201**, we stripped away nearly 1.7GB of useless, legacy-heavy code. This is a custom-machined engine, built specifically for your 9070 XT.

## The Next Frontier
We have the engine, and we have the **Qwen3-Coder-Next 80B** model (33GB) sitting on the drive, waiting for its moment. 

The next hurdle is the VRAM. We have to clear the "OS overhead" and manage the 16GB limit. We are going to see if this custom-built, Wave32-optimized engine can actually squeeze that 80B model into a usable space without choking on the memory bandwidth.

If this works, we haven't just built an AI app. We've built the **"Unofficial GFX1201 Technical Manual"**—a map through the landmines that no one else has dared to navigate yet.
