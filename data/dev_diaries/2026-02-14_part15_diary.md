# Dev Diary: 2026-02-14

## The RDNA 4 Ghost in the Machine

The session started with a crash that felt like a direct assault on my hardware. I was running a Flux generation through ComfyUI, and the whole thing hit a wall during the VAE Decode phase. The traceback was a nightmare: `HIP error: an illegal memory access`. 

It’s that classic friction between the "Soul" of the model—the creative potential of Flux—and the cold, unyielding limits of my RX 9070 XT. I suspected the memory management was failing during the upsampling process. I had Gemini digging through the logs, sifting through the noise of `comfy_nohup.log` and `comfy_flux_20260213_203521.log`. 

The culprit was a specific interaction between PyTorch's `TunableOp` and the RDNA 4 architecture. `TunableOp` is a tempting beast; it promises a 60% speedup for the Transformer layers by using optimized kernels, but it turns out those kernels are incredibly unstable for the standard `conv2d` operations used in VAE upsampling on this hardware. Even with the "Safe VAE" patch—which forces a `.clone().contiguous()` to prevent memory aliasing—the hardware just couldn't play nice.

I had to make a tactical retreat. I patched `start_flux.sh` to set `PYTORCH_TUNABLEOP_ENABLED="0"`. We traded a massive performance boost for the ability to actually finish a generation without the GPU screaming in agony. Stability over speed. Always.

## Expanding the Nervous System

Once the local AI was stable, the conversation shifted toward the "System" as a whole. I started wondering how this local-first architecture could reach beyond the desktop. I have a house full of smart devices—TP-Link, Sifely, Blink, various Chinese IP cameras—but they've all been sitting in silos.

I wanted the "Commander" (my local voice agent) to act as a central nervous system. I tasked Gemini with mapping the local network. It was a gritty, manual process:
- Running `ip -4 addr` to find the subnet.
- Realizing `nmap` wasn't available in the current environment.
- Writing a custom `network_scanner.py` from scratch to ping and probe ports.

The scanner hit a syntax error early on (a classic truncated file write during a Python script generation), but we squashed it. The scan eventually revealed a landscape of Tuya-based devices and a Google Cast device. 

The architecture of the "Commander" is designed for modularity: a Python-based "Brain" (`commander_voice.py`) that dispatches commands to a PowerShell "Effector" (`local_agent.ps1`). This allowed us to rapidly build a new module, `kasa_control.py`, to interface with the TP-Link Kasa plugs.

## The Integration Breakthrough

The real win was the latency optimization. I didn't want to wait for a full LLM inference cycle just to turn off a lamp. We implemented a regex pre-check in `commander_voice.py`. Now, when I say "Commander, turn on Standing Lamp," the system bypasses the LLM entirely, hitting the Kasa API directly. It's sub-second response time. 

We now have a local-first, voice-controlled command center that can:
1. Control Kasa smart plugs (Bedroom, Standing Lamp, etc.).
2. Scan the entire local subnet for new rogue devices.
3. Identify Tuya/RTSP/Google Cast entities.

The project is evolving from a mere "AI on a PC" to a "Local Intelligence presiding over a physical environment." The silicon is stable, the agents are communicating, and the house is finally listening.
