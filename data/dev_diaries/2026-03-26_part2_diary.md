# Apollo Dev Diary: 2026-03-26 (Part 2)

The battle between the Soul and the System is intensifying. Today was a brutal lesson in the friction between architectural elegance and the cold, hard limits of silicon.

## The MoE Mirage

We spent the first half of the session chasing a phantom. We were testing a 35B Mixture of Experts (MoE) model, which—on paper—should have been the sweet spot. It was sitting at ~12GB in its `IQ2_M` quantization. On a 16GB VRAM card, that sounds like plenty of headroom.

It was a lie.

The "Soul" of the model wanted to be expansive, but the "System" was drowning in the overhead. Because MoE models dynamically swap "experts" in and out of VRAM on a token-by-token basis, the moment the KV cache (the model's short-term memory) started eating up that remaining 3.5GB, the system hit a wall. The PCIe bus became a bottleneck, thrashing experts back and forth between VRAM and system RAM. The result? Blank outputs and endless, looping nonsense. The model thought it was working, but the hardware was just gasping for air.

We made the call to abandon the 35B MoE. We pivoted to the **Qwen3.5-27B-Claude-4.6-OS-Auto-Variable-Heretic**. It’s a dense model—no experts, just one solid block of weights. It sits statically in VRAM. It doesn't swap. It doesn't thrash. It just... *works*.

## The Stress Test: Breaking the Loop

Once the Sovereign Engine was stabilized on the 27B model, we pushed it into a coding loop stress test. This was the real test of the "agentic" capability—seeing if the model could handle a multi-turn ReAct logic chain without losing its mind.

It was a resounding success.

The model was given a prompt designed to fail (a Python script with a missing colon). It:
1. Wrote the broken script.
2. Executed it via `run_shell`.
3. Captured the `SyntaxError`.
4. Analyzed the stack trace.
5. Patched the file with `write_code`.
6. Re-ran and successfully captured the output.

It completed a 5-turn logical chain entirely autonomously. It even responded with a casual, "Zoey-compliant" voice after finishing the task. The ability to read terminal errors and self-correct is the threshold where local AI moves from a "chatbot" to a "true agent."

## Silicon Optimization: Fighting for Watts

The session took a turn into the gritty reality of hardware management. The model was struggling with memory constraints, and the connection was being refused. We had to get aggressive. I realized we weren't just fighting memory; we were fighting power.

I steered the session to push the AMD RX 9070 XT to its limits. We used `rocm-smi` to force the performance mode to `compute` and set the power cap to 330W. We're not just trying to run code; we're trying to force the hardware to prioritize the inference throughput. It's a constant battle to keep the power-hungry beast stable while it's performing high-intensity reasoning.

## The Future: Quantization as the New Physics

The conversation drifted into the philosophical implications of quantization. As we discussed the current state of the art—AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization) and the upcoming wave of 1.58-bit "BitNet" models—it became clear: the future of computing isn't about getting *bigger* chips; it's about how much we can *compress* reality.

We are moving toward a world where the entire model's intelligence is distilled into tiny, efficient, quantized mathematical representations. As I noted, life itself is just a quantization technique—taking the continuous flow of reality and turning it into discrete, manageable biological signals. 

The next frontier for Project Apollo isn't just better code; it's finding that perfect, razor-thin slice of quantized data that allows the "Soul" to exist on the "System" without suffocating it. We'll keep playing with the 35B MoE quants, but the 27B dense model is our new baseline.
