# Dev Diary: The Electromagnetic Ghost in the Machine

**January 22, 2026**

Today was a relentless battle against signal integrity and the fickle nature of electromagnetic induction. I’ve spent the better part of the day wrestling with the BTT Eddy sensor, and the friction between the "Soul" (the dream of a perfectly level, high-speed printing machine) and the "System" (the stubborn, noisy reality of the silicon) has never been more palpable.

The core of the problem was a persistent, maddening I2C communication error. I was chasing a "NACK" (Negative Acknowledge) error that seemed to appear only when the system actually tried to *work*. I initially thought it was a hardware defect or a bad flash on the RP2040, but the deeper I dug, the more I realized it was a fundamental mismatch between the firmware and the hardware's electrical characteristics.

The breakthrough came when I realized the `reg_drive_current` was the culprit. The LDC1612 sensor chip is incredibly sensitive; the automatic calibration was attempting to set a drive current that the MCU couldn't maintain stably. By manually bumping that value—moving the current from 17 to 18—the communication finally stabilized. It was a reminder that in the world of local-first AI and high-precision hardware, "close enough" isn't enough. The physics of the sensor must align perfectly with the logic of the firmware.

Once the I2C-bus ghost was exorcized, the real test began: the Z-axis tilt calibration. This was the moment of truth for the Tri-Z architecture. I watched the terminal as the bed attempted to level itself. The initial readings were absurd—a 4.3mm range. The bed was essentially a cliff. But the iterative logic of the Klipper calibration held. It fought the tilt, correcting the Z1 and Z2 motors through multiple passes, and finally, it converged to a range of 0.0019mm. 

The precision is staggering, but the "System" is still fighting back. Even with a perfectly level bed, the Z-offset is drifting slightly, likely due to the thermal expansion of the probe itself. It’s a reminder that even when we achieve mathematical perfection, the physical world is always drifting.

**Technical Lessons Learned:**

* **I2C Stability:** In high-speed/high-frequency sensor tasks, the `reg_drive_current` is not just a tuning parameter; it is the difference between a functioning system and a shutdown loop. 
* **Firmware-Hardware Interplay:** The suggestion to use the `GENERIC_03H with CLKDIV 4` flash setting for the RP2040 is non-negotiable for the Eddy sensor. The clock divider is crucial for stabilizing the communication between the MCU and the LDC chip.
* **The Architecture of Precision:** The success of the `Z_TILT_ADJUST` confirms that the Tri-Z geometry is sound. The convergence of the bed level proves the software-hardware handshake is working, even if the thermal drift remains a challenge for the next session.

**Next Steps:**
The hardware is stabilized, but the software is still hungry. I need to tackle the thermal compensation next. If I can't map the temperature-induced inductance drift, the Eddy sensor's precision will be lost to the wind as soon as the chamber warms up.

The printer is currently "alive," but it's a skeleton. It needs the skin (the panels) and the heat (the calibration) before it can truly live.
