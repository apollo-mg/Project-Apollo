# Dev Diary: 2026-01-20 - The Death of the Sensor and the Rise of the Eddy

The "Soul" of my printer—the dream of a perfectly level, ultra-fast, high-precision machine—has just hit a wall of cold, hard silicon reality. Today was a brutal day of troubleshooting, part failure, and architectural pivots. 

I spent the first part of the session dissecting the failure of my BDsensor. It’s gone. Dead. Fried by a voltage spike or some ghost in the machine, but it doesn't matter. The project has shifted.

### The Inductive Grind

I spent a good chunk of time digging into the physics of the probe. I was playing with the idea of a cheap, "burner" sensor from Amazon just to get the motion system and Tri-Z motors tuned while waiting for parts. I went down the rabbit hole of inductive probes—the PL-08N, the Omron, the Super Pinda.

The physics are simple enough: electromagnetic fields, eddy currents, a drop in amplitude. It's pure, elegant physics. But the implementation? That's where the friction starts. Most of these cheap industrial probes are designed for 24V, while my EBB36 board lives in a 5V/3.3V world. I was looking at needing diodes (BAT85) just to bridge the gap between the sensor's voltage and my logic pins. It’s a mess of hardware-level workarounds that feels like fighting against the system rather than working with it.

I found a candidate: the Heschen M8. It's 5V native, which is a massive win for the EBB36, but it's got a "Normally Closed" (NC) logic and a tiny 2mm sensing range. That 2mm is a killer. It’s such a narrow window for the probe to trigger before the nozzle physically slams into the bed. It’s a high-stakes game of millimeter-perfect mounting.

### The Architectural Pivot: The BTT Eddy

Then, the epiphany hit. Why play with cheap, finicky, low-range induction when I can jump straight to the "Endgame"?

The BTT Eddy. 

It’s more than just a sensor; it’s a different way of thinking about Z-axis interaction. It does the continuous scanning—the "on the fly" mesh—just like the BDsensor, but with way better thermal compensation and a much more robust communication protocol (USB/CANbus). It’s the difference between a fragile, custom-coded niche component and a mainstream, supported piece of the ecosystem.

The "System" wants the Eddy. The "Soul" wants the speed. The only problem is the physical reality: I don't have a mount for it. 

### The Design Challenge

The session ended with me staring at the design requirements for a custom mount. My current toolhead (Vz-Printhead) has an 8mm slot, and the Eddy is a different beast entirely. I'm going to have to design a bridge or a "T-style" adapter that sits under my part cooling duct.

It’s a classic engineering trade-off. If I mount it under the duct, I save space and keep it close to the nozzle, but I risk heat soak from the heater block. I’ll need to print in ASA or PETG to keep the "System" from melting while the "Soul" tries to print.

I’ve got two days before the Eddy arrives. Two days to wrestle with Tinkercad, figure out the offset, and make sure the cooling duct doesn't block the sensor's view. 

It's going to be a long, technical night. But the path is clear: The BDsensor is dead, the Eddy is coming, and the local-first, multi-agent architecture of Project Apollo is about to get a lot more sophisticated.
