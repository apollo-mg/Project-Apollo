# Dev Diary: 2025-11-21
## Part 18 — Illegal Memory Access, Bitwise Betrayal, and the Silicon That Hates Me

---

### The Crash That Won't End

Three AM. The terminal is my confessional.

It started with a promise: `uni_pc` sampler, `Wan2.1-T2V-14B`, the works. A cinematic drone shot of a futuristic city at sunset, neon lights bleeding across flying cars. The kind of prompt that makes you forget you're wrestling with 16 gigabytes of temperamental memory on a GPU that doesn't play nice with PyTorch's CUDA masquerade.

Then the knife:

```
torch.AcceleratorError: HIP error: an illegal memory access was encountered
...
File "/home/mark/gemini/ai/ComfyUI/comfy/model_management.py", line 1082, in cast_to
    r.copy_(weight, non_blocking=non_blocking)
```

An *illegal memory access*. Not out of bounds—those I can respect, those I can debug. No, this is the GPU's way of saying "I touched something I wasn't allowed to touch, and I'm not apologizing."

---

### The Hunt for the Ghost in dequant.py

The stack trace pointed everywhere and nowhere. `uni_pc.py` → `model_management.py` → `cast_to_device` → the abyss.

But the pattern was there if you knew where to look: the crash happened during weight transfers, specifically during GGUF dequantization. The `ComfyUI-GGUF` extension was loading `Wan2.1-T2V-14B-Q4_K_M.gguf`—a 14-billion parameter model, quantized to Q4_K, which should have been a *blessing* for my measly 16 GB.

Except GGUF quantization has a dark secret.

The dequantization functions—`dequantize_blocks_Q4_K`, `dequantize_blocks_Q6_K`, and their siblings—perform bitwise operations on temporary tensors. They create masks, they shift bits, they do arithmetic that assumes the GPU's memory controller behaves like the one the author tested on.

On my RX 9070 XT (RDNA 4, architecture `gfx1201`), it does not.

---

### The Patch That Felt Like Surgery

I opened `dequant.py`. The culprit wasn't obvious at first glance. The code looked correct—allocate a tensor, apply a bitwise shift, free it. But somewhere in there, a temporary allocation was leaking, or overlapping, or triggering a race condition that ROCm's asynchronous kernel execution would eventually expose.

I started with `dequantize_blocks_Q4_K`. The pattern:

```python
# The suspect operation (simplified)
mask = (some_tensor >> shift_amount) & 0b1111
```

Bitwise shifts on GPU tensors should be innocent. They're *inert*. But when combined with non-blocking memory transfers (`non_blocking=True`), when combined with ROCm's peculiarities around pinned memory, when combined with the fact that I was running at 3 AM with too much coffee and not enough sleep...

I manually reallocated the tensors. I forced synchronization. I added explicit device placement. It felt like patching a kernel panic with duct tape and prayers.

Then I extended it to `dequantize_blocks_Q6_K`, then to every dequantization function that touched temporary memory. The logs showed mixed quantization types in the model—Q4_K (280 layers), Q6_K (120 layers), F32 (695 layers). Every one of them a potential failure point.

---

### The Sampler Switch

Even with the patch, `uni_pc` remained suspicious. It crashed too cleanly, too predictably. So I made a trade:

**Sacrifice the sampler, save the pipeline.**

I switched to `dpmpp_2m` with a `simple` scheduler. It's slower. It's less elegant. But it doesn't crash.

The `ModelSamplingFlux` node remained, with its shift parameters (`max_shift=3`, `base_shift=1`) to prevent the "disjointed frames" plague that had cursed earlier attempts. Video generation is a balancing act between coherence and chaos, and sometimes you need to nudge the noise schedule to remind the model what continuity means.

---

### The 16 GB Problem

Then came the question: *Can we run the full Wan 2.2 workflow on 16 GB of VRAM?*

The reference workflow (`video_wan2_2_14B_t2v.json`) was built for systems that didn't sweat the small stuff. Let's do the math:

- **UNET (14B params, full precision)**: ~28 GB in FP16, ~14 GB in FP8
- **CLIP (UMT5-XXL)**: ~2-3 GB, or ~1 GB with FP8 scaling
- **VAE**: ~500 MB
- **Latent space (720×544×16 frames)**: ~100 MB
- **Activations, gradients, overhead**: The rest of your soul

Total: ~35-40 GB for a clean run.

I have 16.

The solution isn't pretty. It's GGUF quantization (Q4_K_M, accepting the dequantization headaches). It's FP8 scaling for the text encoder (`umt5_xxl_fp8_e4m3fn_scaled.safetensors`). It's aggressive offloading to CPU when VRAM runs hot. It's accepting that some workflows will never be *stable*, only *temporarily not crashing*.

---

### Lessons Carved in Stack Traces

**1. AMD ROCm is not NVIDIA CUDA wearing a hat.** The HIP compatibility layer is impressive, but it's not perfect. Illegal memory accesses that never trigger on NVIDIA will happily demolish your pipeline on RDNA. Test on the metal you intend to ship on.

**2. Quantization is a double-edged sword.** GGUF saves VRAM, yes. But the dequantization math is fragile, and the community's error handling is... aspirational. When you load a quantized model, you're trusting strangers' bitwise operations with your production pipeline.

**3. Samplers matter more than you think.** `uni_pc` crashed. `dpmpp_2m` lived. The difference wasn't in my code—it was in how each sampler orchestrates memory during the diffusion steps. Some samplers are well-behaved guests. Others bring a entourage.

**4. The "disjointed frames" bug is a feature of under-specified noise schedules.** The `ModelSamplingFlux` shift parameters aren't a hack; they're a calibration. Video diffusion needs temporal coherence, and sometimes you have to nudge the model's understanding of time.
