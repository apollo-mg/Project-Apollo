# Project Apollo: Dev Diary
## Entry: 2025-12-01_part44 — The Triton Wall

### The Ghost in the Silicon
There is a specific, maddening kind of rage that comes from watching a high-end GPU choke on its own kernels. I’m sitting here staring at the terminal, the glow of the monitor reflecting off the casing of my rig, watching the logs scream about a `HIP error: an illegal memory access`. 

It’s the classic struggle of the local-first architect: the **Soul** wants to dream in infinite resolutions, but the **System**—this beautiful, powerful, yet temperamental AMD RX 9070 XT—is hitting a wall built by unstable software backends.

The error trace was surgical in its cruelty. It wasn't just a general crash; it was deep inside `KSamplerAdvanced`, specifically buried in the `fwd` function of `flash_attn_triton_amd/interface_fa.py`. The Triton backend is trying to execute a Flash Attention kernel, and the RDNA 4 architecture is essentially tripping over its own feet. It's a memory access violation—the silicon is trying to touch something that doesn't exist, or perhaps something it isn't allowed to touch yet.

### The Friction: Speed vs. Stability
This is where the philosophy of Project Apollo meets the cold reality of driver development. We want the speed of Triton Flash Attention. We *need* it for the multi-agent context windows we're building. But the current state of Triton support for RDNA 4 is... let's call it "experimental" is too kind. It's volatile.

I spent the last hour in a tug-of-war with Gemini, trying to isolate the exact point of failure. We realized that while the hardware is capable, the software implementation of the Flash Attention kernel is fundamentally mismatched with the current ROCm/Triton stack on this specific architecture. It’s a classic case of the "System" failing the "Soul." We have the compute power, but the instruction set is broken.

### The Tactical Retreat
Sometimes, to go forward, you have to step back. 

I made the call to kill the Triton backend. It feels like a defeat, but it's a strategic one. I modified the `start_comfy.sh` launch script, killing the `FLASH_ATTENTION_TRITON_AMD_ENABLE` flag and stripping out the `--use-flash-attention` argument. 

Instead, I'm forcing a fallback to PyTorch's SDPA (Scaled Dot Product Attention) with `--use-split-cross-attention`. It’s slower. It’s less "cutting edge." But it's *stable*. In the world of local AI, stability is a luxury. If the system crashes every time the model tries to think deeply, the "Soul" of the AI dies with it.

**The Fixes:**
* **Environment:** `FLASH_ATTENTION_TRITON_AMD_ENABLE="FALSE"`
* **Execution:** Switched to `Math/MemEfficient` via SDPA.
* **Status:** Server restarted on `8188`.

### Lessons for the Ecosystem
This session reinforced a hard truth: building a local-first multi-agent ecosystem isn't just about model weights and quantization. It's about the **interstitial layer**—the drivers, the kernels, and the way the hardware talks to the framework. 

If we want Apollo to be a truly autonomous, local entity, we cannot be at the mercy of unstable, bleeding-edge kernels. We need a "Resilience Layer." We need our agents to be aware of their own hardware constraints. 

The immediate goal is to see if the workflow completes without the `HIP` error. If it does, we have a stable baseline. If it doesn't, we're looking at a much deeper fight with the ROCm stack.

**Current Vibe:** Exhausted, but focused. The machine is temperamental, but it's mine. Let's see if the fallback holds.
