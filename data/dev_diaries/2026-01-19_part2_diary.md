# Dev Diary: 2026-01-19

## The Z-Axis Ghost in the Machine

I thought I had finally mastered the physical dance of the Tri-Z. The motors were moving, the kinematics were corrected, and the bed was finally responding to the bed-tilt commands. It felt like the "System" was finally yielding to the "Soul" of the architecture. I was just a few steps away from a clean Bed Mesh.

Then, the silicon bit back.

### The Kinematic Correction
The morning started with a classic coordinate inversion. My Z motors were moving "UP" when the firmware expected "DOWN." It was a simple enough fix—flipping the `dir_pin` logic—but it revealed the fragility of the configuration. Once corrected, the `STEPPER_BUZZ` test confirmed the bed was finally moving in the correct Cartesian direction. 

The real challenge, however, was the leveling. The `Z_TILT_ADJUST` was struggling. It was attempting to level the bed, but it was fighting its own shadow. The probe readings were wildly divergent, with the error magnitude increasing after every attempt. 

### The Pivot Point Epiphany
The error was a classic mapping failure. I had assumed the driver order matched the physical geometry. It didn't. The "Rear" motor was actually the "Front Right" motor, and vice versa. I had to perform a surgical swap of the Z1 and Z2 definitions in the config to match the actual hardware-to-driver mapping. This is the recurring friction of local-first development: you can have the perfect math, but if the silicon is wired to the wrong port, the math is useless.

### The Sensor Paradox
The transition to the BDsensor (the "Eddy" style sensor) was meant to be the leap into high-speed, local-first intelligence. No more physical probing; just pure, electromagnetic distance sensing. 

But the sensor wouldn't talk. `M102 S-6` was failing, reporting "Too Far" even when the nozzle was practically touching the steel plate. I suspected an I2C signal integrity issue—common in these high-speed, low-voltage environments. I attempted to add a pull-up resistor (`^`) to the SDA line via software to stabilize the signal. 

The `M102 S-1` command finally returned the firmware version (`V1.2c`), confirming that the sensor and the EBB toolhead were finally shaking hands. The connection was established. The data was there. The dream of high-speed, contactless leveling was finally within reach.

### The Hardware Tragedy
Then, the crash. 

In an attempt to troubleshoot a loose connection, I made a momentary error. A momentary bridge of 5V into the SCL line. In the world of microcontrollers, there is no such thing as a "minor" electrical mistake. 

The sensor didn't just stop working; it began to bleed heat. The "Soul" of the sensor—the delicate, high-frequency electromagnetic field it's meant to generate—has been scorched by the very power meant to sustain it. 

### Lessons Learned
1. **Configuration is not just logic; it is physical mapping.** If your hardware-to-software mapping isn't 1:1, the most advanced AI/Firmware will fail.
2. **The sensor is the heartbeat of the system.** The BDsensor was supposed to be the bridge between the digital mesh and the physical bed, but it's now a broken component.
3. **Silicon is unforgiving.** In the quest for "Local-First" speed, we are working at the edge of voltage tolerances.

The project is stalled. I need a new BD sensor, a new cable, and a fresh start. The architecture is sound, but the hardware is currently a casualty of my own curiosity.

**Next Steps:** Order replacement BD sensor. Re-verify EBB toolhead wiring. Pray for the Octopus.
