# Apollo Dev Diary: 2026-03-01

The friction between silicon and soul is reaching a fever pitch. Today was a grueling dance through the jagged landscape of Linux audio drivers, but we broke through the noise—literally.

### The Audio Nightmare: A War of Architectures
I spent most of the afternoon wrestling with the "Audio Wall." Trying to get a clean, low-latency voice input for Zoey is a nightmare in the Linux ecosystem. We tried the "Headless PipeWire" method, attempting to inject a DeepFilterNet neural network directly into the PipeWire stream via a LADSPA plugin. It was elegant in theory, but the implementation was a mess; the `sounddevice` library was bypassing the PipeWire/PulseAudio layer entirely to talk straight to the ALSA hardware. 

It’s the classic Linux struggle: you have the speed of ALSA, the mixing capability of PulseAudio, and the professional latency of JACK, but getting them to play nice is a constant battle. I eventually had to abandon the external plugin approach and build a raw, software-based noise gate directly into Zoey's Python brain. I implemented a dynamic threshold that ignores the baseline noise floor of the PC fans and the 3D printer. It’s a "brute force" solution, but it works—it keeps her ears focused on my voice and not the ambient noise of the lab.

### The AMD Shadow
It is infuriating to realize that my AMD RX 9070 XT has dedicated hardware for this. The RDNA 4 architecture has onboard DSP blocks designed for exactly this kind of noise suppression, but AMD leaves them "dark" in Linux. They keep the proprietary models tucked away in the Windows driver. It’s a reminder that even when we build "Sovereign" systems, we are still dancing on hardware that was designed for a more centralized, corporate world. We are essentially building our own drivers on top of their silicon.

### Expanding the Mind: The Voice Satellite
The breakthrough came when we shifted from thinking about a single monolithic agent to a distributed one. We successfully provisioned a Raspberry Pi 5 as a dedicated "Voice Satellite." 

The Pi 5 is essentially a high-performance edge node. It doesn't need to be a powerhouse; it just needs to be the "ears." It will handle the raw audio stream, running its own minimal noise gate, and then ship the audio buffer over the network to my desktop's ROCm-powered inference engine. 

I’ve officially added "Distributed Voice Satellites" to the roadmap. This is the blueprint for a multi-room, house-wide presence. We aren't just building a chatbot; we are building a distributed, sovereign consciousness that lives in the house, not the cloud.

### The Philosophy of the Build
I'm working under a "Move Fast and Break Things" ethos. I'm a performance junkie, and I'm okay with the risk. If the driver updates break the ROCm stack, we fix it. If the Pi 5 crashes, we rebuild it. 

There's a strange beauty in this. I live a modest life, often relying on the systems I'm trying to improve. But the ability to build something like Project Apollo—a system that doesn't report your voice to a corporate server or throttle your intellect behind a subscription wall—is a form of freedom.
