# Dev Diary: The Ghost in the Kernel (2025-12-03)

The bleeding edge isn't a sharp tool; it’s a jagged, unpredictable mess. 

Tonight was one of those sessions where the hardware feels less like a facilitator and more like a saboteur. We're pushing the RDNA 4 architecture on this 9070 XT, trying to get Wan 2.1 to breathe, but instead, we got a video that seemed to be trying to travel back in time.

### The Temporal Glitch

It started with the output. We were running the Wan 2.1 model, expecting fluid, high-fidelity video motion, but what we got was... wrong. The motion was reversed. It wasn't just a bad seed; the temporal flow was fundamentally broken.

I was digging through the logs with Gemini, trying to trace the madness. We initially suspected the causal masking—the logic that tells the model "don't look at the future while predicting the present." If the `causal` flag was flipped incorrectly in the Flash Attention wrapper, the model would lose its sense of sequence. But even after we checked the RoPE (Rotary Positional Embedding) implementation, the video was still playing backwards.

That’s when the realization hit. It wasn't a logical error in the code. It was a mathematical hallucination.

### Silent Failures: The RDNA 4 Problem

This is the nightmare scenario for any architect: **The Silent Failure.** 

If the system crashes with an `Out of Memory` or a `Segmentation Fault`, you know where you stand. You fix the VRAM issue, you move on. But when the code *runs* without throwing a single error, yet the output is total garbage, you’re hunting a ghost.

The culprit appears to be the Triton kernel. We're using Flash Attention to squeeze every bit of performance out of the 9070 XT, but it seems the compiled kernel is fundamentally misbehaving on the RDNA 4 instruction set. My hunch? A mismatch between the expected Wave64 execution (common on older AMD gear) and the Wave32 architecture of RDNA 4. 

The GPU was performing the GEMM (General Matrix Multiply) operations, but it was likely mishandling thread synchronization or memory ordering. It was writing the results to memory in the wrong order—scrambling the temporal dimensions of the video. The "backwards" motion wasn't a creative choice by the model; it was the hardware literally writing the frames in a scrambled sequence.

**The fix was a retreat.** We had to abandon the speed of Flash Attention and fall back to **Split Attention**. It’s slower, heavier on the VRAM, and lacks that "instant" feel, but it’s numerically stable. In the struggle between the *Soul* (the creative vision of the video) and the *System* (the silicon's broken math), the System won this round. We chose "Slow and Correct" over "Fast and Broken."

### Scaling the Preview

A side note: even after we stabilized the attention mechanism, we hit a minor "visual" hurdle. The KSampler was outputing these tiny, strangely aspect-ratioed previews (512x294) despite my latent being set to a much larger 1280x736. 

A quick dive into the ComfyUI internals revealed the standard `--preview-size` behavior. The system was aggressively downscaling the preview to save bandwidth. It’s a classic case of the system prioritizing efficiency over the user's need to actually see what’s happening. We patched the `start_comfy.sh` script to force a higher preview resolution. It’s a small thing, but when you’re monitoring a massive generation, seeing a blurry mess is maddening.

### Lessons from the Trenches

1.  **The "Arrow of Time" is expensive:** On new hardware like RDNA 4, the most expensive thing isn't memory—it's reliability. We're still in the "Wild West" phase where ROCm is catching up to the hardware.
2.  **Beware of "Optimization":** Flash Attention is the holy grail, but if the kernel doesn't respect your architecture's wave-front logic, it's just a very fast way to generate noise.
3.  **The Pivot is Mandatory:** A good architect knows when to stop fighting the kernel and just use a different path. Split Attention is our "Safe Harbor" until the official `pytorch-rocm` wheels catch up.

The machine is finally behaving. The motion is moving forward. Now, we just have to see if we can make it move beautifully.
