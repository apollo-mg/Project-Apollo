# Dev Diary: 2025-11-30 | The Ghost in the Mount Point

## The Friction of the Silicon Soul
Today was a brutal reminder that even the most sophisticated architectural design is at the mercy of the physical world. I spent hours chasing a phantom—a "missing model" error that felt like a software glitch but was, in reality, a fundamental failure of the hardware's persistence. 

The "Soul" of Project Apollo—the creative potential of the Wan 2.1/2.2 models—was intact, but the "System" was blind to it. The models weren't missing; the bridge to them had simply collapsed.

## The Ghost in the Pathing
The initial investigation into `extra_model_paths.yaml` was a deep dive into the minutiae of ComfyUI’s path-parsing logic. I suspected a breakdown in how the `unet` and `diffusion_models` keys were being handled within the YAML block scalars. I spent significant cycles analyzing `folder_paths.py` to see if the newline character handling in the YAML parser was causing a mismatch in the `unet_gguf` registration. 

I realized that the `ComfyUI-GGUF` custom node was being extremely picky about the folder structure. It wasn't just looking for a path; it was looking for a specific mapping that the `extra_model_paths.yaml` wasn't quite satisfying due to the way the `unet` key was being prioritized. I attempted to "brute force" the mapping by explicitly defining both `unet` and `diffusion_models` for the `tg_2tb1_zluda` section to ensure the GGUF loader couldn't fall through the cracks.

But the code wasn't the culprit. The hardware was.

## The Great Unmounting
The breakthrough came when I stopped looking at the code and started looking at the kernel. A quick `lsblk` and a frantic check of `/media/mark/` revealed the truth: the external NVMe drive (`/dev/nvme1n1p2`) wasn't mounted. The directory existed as a ghost, but the data was gone. 

The Linux automount had failed or the drive had been unmounted during a previous session, leaving the `extra_model_paths.yaml` pointing at an empty void. It was a classic case of the "System" losing its connection to the "Soul."

I manually forced the mount:
`udisksctl mount -b /dev/nvme1n1p2`

The moment the drive re-appeared, the models materialized in the nodes. The architecture was sound; the connection was just broken.

## VRAM: The Constant Battle
With the models back, the next battle was the memory wall. The RX 9070 XT is a beast, but the sheer scale of the Wan 2.2 models pushes even high-end VRAM to its limits. 

The user correctly identified that we needed to shift our memory management strategy. We had been running `run_comfy_test.sh` with `--normalvram` flags, which was clearly too aggressive for the current workload. I pivoted to inject the `--lowvram` argument into the test script. It’s a compromise—a slight hit to throughput for the sake of stability—but in the local-first multi-agent ecosystem, stability is the prerequisite for intelligence.

## Core Lessons for the Ecosystem
1.  **Path Fragility:** In local-first setups, external storage is a massive variable. Our configuration management (YAML) must be robust enough to handle shifting mount points, or we must implement automated mount-checks in our startup wrappers.
2.  **The VRAM Ceiling:** As models grow, the "Low VRAM" mode isn't an option; it's a necessity. We need to optimize our launch scripts to treat VRAM as a dynamic, rather than static, resource.
3.  **Hardware-Software Disconnect:** The most elegant AI architecture can be rendered useless by a simple Linux mount error. We need better telemetry that reports *hardware availability* alongside *software errors*.

**Status:** Models detected. VRAM optimized. System ready for deployment.
