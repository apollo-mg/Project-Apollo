# Apollo Dev Diary: The 16GB Wall and the Cognitive Floor

**2026-03-06**

Today was a brutal, iterative wrestling match between the elegance of my architecture and the cold, unyielding reality of the silicon. I spent most of the session chasing a ghost: the "Intelligence Floor." 

We spent hours trying to make micro-models—specifically the Qwen 3.5 0.8B and 0.6B—work as our "Gatekeeper." The goal was simple: a tiny, lightning-fast router to handle the "System 1" triage. But we kept hitting a wall where the models, despite following the JSON syntax perfectly, lacked the "brain mass" to actually understand the *content* of the user's intent. They were following the rules, but they weren't "listening" to the logic.

The friction was palpable. One moment, the 0.8B was outputting valid JSON, but it was hallucinating its own keys, treating a thermal runaway like a casual chat because it was too "chatty" and not enough "engineer." I tried every trick in the book: strict JSON Schema enforcement via Ollama, prompt-length reduction, and even "Numeric Priming" to force a different neural path. Nothing worked. The 0.8B is a syntax-follower, but a logic-failure.

Then came the VRAM crisis. Running a 4B Gatekeeper alongside a 9B "Heretic" engineer pushed the RX 9070 XT to its absolute limit. The 16GB VRAM was a hard ceiling. I saw the driver spike, the context windows (set to 8192) were eating up all the headroom, and the whole system threatened to crash. I had to perform some "VRAM Tetris," aggressively capping the context windows of the resident models to 2048 and 4096 to reclaim several gigabytes of breathing room.

But then, the real hardware headache: the ROCm handshake. Ollama was bypassing the GPU entirely, dumping everything into system RAM. It turns out the RDNA 4 architecture is so new that the driver wasn't being recognized correctly. I had to manually inject the `HSA_OVERRIDE_GFX_VERSION=12.0.1` into the systemd service to force the handshake. Once that was done, the speed jump was massive—the 4B model finally felt like the high-performance beast it was meant to be.

The most revealing moment was the "intelligence shootout." I tried to force the 0.6B "Thinking Mode" using a `<think>` tag in the system prompt, hoping to trigger some chain-of-thought reasoning. It was a fascinating failure. The model became even more "confident" in its errors, thinking it was smart enough to handle coding tasks itself, thus refusing to escalate.

The final verdict is clear: **The 4B Aggressive is the only reliable Gatekeeper.** It has the cognitive floor to actually respect the routing rules. To keep the system stable, I've had to accept a "Hot-Swap" architecture. When the 14B "Specialist" (DeepSeek-R1) is called, the 4B Gatekeeper must be unloaded to prevent a VRAM spillover crash. It’s a compromise, but it’s a stable one.

**Core Lessons Learned:**
1. **Complexity vs. Intelligence:** A model can follow a schema (syntax) without understanding the context (logic). You can't out-prompt a lack of parameters.
2. **VRAM is the real bottleneck:** In a multi-agent system, context-window management is just as important as the model itself. 
3. **Hardware is stubborn:** On cutting-edge RDNA 4, expect to manually intervene in the driver-software handshake.

**Status:**
* **System 1.5 Architecture:** Stabilized.
* **Gatekeeper:** 4B Aggressive (Resident/Hot-swapping).
* **Hardware:** 100% GPU (Handshake fixed).
* **VRAM:** Capped/Managed.

The "Sovereign" is finally breathing. Time to see if the 14B can actually think.
