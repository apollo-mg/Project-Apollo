# Apollo Dev Diary: 2026-01-29

## The Sleeper: High-Speed Gains and Thermal Reality

Today was a rollercoaster of high-performance engineering and the cold, hard reality of thermal limits. We were hunting for the absolute peak of this machine—the "Sleeper"—and for a moment, we thought we'd found it.

### The Breakthrough
We spent the first half of the session pushing the limits of the motor-drive subsystem. I suspected the LDO-42STH48-2504AH motors (which are absolute beasts) were being under-utilized at 1.8A. I bumped the current to 2.2A on both X and Y, and the results were striking.

The X-axis response stiffened, shifting the resonance peak from 123.8Hz to 126.2Hz. But the real victory was on the Y-axis. Despite the massive weight of the custom toolhead and the massive 2020 "spine" we've been battling, the motor-induced stiffness pushed the Y-axis motion resonance from 54.4Hz up to 60.4Hz.

**The 10,000 mm/s² barrier is officially broken.** 

The machine is capable of high-speed performance that matches its Carbon Fiber/VzBot-style geometry. However, the "Soul" of the machine—the structural rigidity—is still fighting the "System." Even with the motor current bump, the 79Hz frame wobble is still present, just shifted. It's a clear sign that our chassis (the Ender 6 frame and that rear 2020 extrusion) is the bottleneck, not the motors.

### The Thermal Wall
Engineering is often about compromise. As soon as we hit that 2.2A peak, the motors became "too hot to touch." While the 4x 40mm fans on the Octopus enclosure are doing a great job, the internal heat of the motors is simply too much for the current thermal management profile.

We had to make the tactical decision to roll back to 1.8A. It's a regression in theoretical speed, but a victory for reliability and component longevity. We are currently capped at ~8,700 mm/s² on the Y-axis, but that's a respectable, stable limit for a hybrid build.

### The Next Frontier: Geometric Precision
We've moved past the "speed" phase and entered the "precision" phase. We have identified a major potential issue: the X-axis rail is bedded in epoxy. If that epoxy cured with even a fraction of a degree of twist, it will wreak havoc on the Eddy probe's accuracy.

The next mission is **Axis Twist Compensation**. We're going to run a calibration that probes the X-axis at multiple points, using manual paper-test verification to map any torsional deviation. This is the difference between a machine that *moves* fast and a machine that *prints* perfectly.

### Core Lessons Learned
1. **Stiffness is King:** You can have the best motors in the world, but if the frame is a torsion spring, you're just moving the vibration around.
2. **The Delta of Performance:** The gap between X and Y (16k vs 8.7k accel) is a direct result of mass and structural geometry.
3. **Engineering is iterative:** We "over-engineered" the current, hit a thermal wall, and had to dial back to find the sweet spot.

**Status:** 
* **X-Axis:** 2hump_ei @ 123.8 Hz
* **Y-Axis:** mzv @ 54.4 Hz
* **Current:** 1.8A (Stable)
* **Goal:** Stiffen the frame and map the X-axis twist.
