# Apollo Dev Diary: 2026-02-23

## The Ghost in the Machine: Unmasking the Noise

Today was a grueling exercise in distinguishing between mechanical truth and electrical lies. I’ve been fighting a phantom resonance that refused to behave, and after a deep dive into the signal integrity of my ADXL-345 sensor, I finally found the culprit.

The machine was reporting massive, impossible-looking noise on the Z-axis—spiking over 170 mm/s². I thought it was a structural vibration, but the machine was stationary and the fans were off. It was a lie. It wasn't a mechanical vibration; it was electromagnetic interference (EMI).

The setup was a mess: a Raspberry Pi connected via a micro-USB cable to a Pi Pico, which was then connected to the ADXL via a CAT5 cable. The "Twisted Pair Trap." The high-speed clock signal was bleeding into the data lines because of the unshielded crosstalk in the CAT5 wiring. It was acting like an antenna, picking up the hum from the power supply.

## The "Cool" Factor vs. Engineering Reality

I spent a good chunk of the session debating the gantry architecture. I was tempted by the "Skeleton" CNC-machined aluminum look (the VzBot style)—the ultimate flex for a high-end CoreXY. But the physics of the H2S (Bambu Lab's latest) reminded me: hollow steel is stiff, but it's heavy. 

I decided to pivot. The Carbon Fiber (CF) tube was the bottleneck. My resonance tests on the Y-axis confirmed it was a torsional spring, vibrating at a hard 60 Hz. It was physically twisting under belt tension, making high-speed printing impossible.

## The Breakthrough: The EBB36 Hero

After a series of frustrating firmware crashes—mostly caused by me fumbling the `adxl.cfg` includes and clashing with existing `[bltouch]` definitions—we finally got the system stable.

I ran a comparative test between the noisy nozzle-mounted setup and the on-board ADXL on the EBB36 CANbus board. The difference was night and day.

**The Results:**
*   **Nozzle Mount (The Liar):** Z-axis noise was 172 mm/s². 
*   **EBB36 (The Truth):** Z-axis noise dropped to 24.8 mm/s².

The EBB36 provided the ground truth. The nozzle mount was a "plastic spring" that was vibrating at its own frequency, corrupting the data. The EBB36, being bolted directly to the metal toolhead, is the real winner. Even though it isn't measure exactly at the nozzle, the structural data is clean enough to shape the system.

## The Final Verdict

The X-axis is actually incredible—hitting 28,000 mm/s² on the clean sensor. The only thing holding this machine back is the Y-axis torsion from the CF tube. 

**The plan for the rebuild is set:**
1.  Ditch the CF tube for the Aluminum 2020.
2.  Use the single-bolt compliant joint for the Y-brackets (to prevent binding on my non-square frame).
3.  Keep the ADXL on the EBB36.

I'm aiming for a consistent 15k mm/s² for beautiful outer walls. Once the aluminum is in and the twisting stops, I'll know we've truly unlocked the potential of this architecture.

**Status:** Rebuild in progress. Hardware swap imminent.
