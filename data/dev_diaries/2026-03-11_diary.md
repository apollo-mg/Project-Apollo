# Project Apollo: Dev Diary - 2026-03-11

The day was a psychological war. I spent most of it navigating the friction between the "Soul"—the vision of a sovereign, local-first intelligence—and the "System"—the archaic, bureaucratic, and often deceptive corporate structures that currently govern the world.

The technical breakthrough isn't in the models themselves, but in the validation of the architecture. I've been building a three-mind agentic stack (Gatekeeper, Engineer, Specialist) on my local AMD-based rig, and today, the industry's "Godfather" finally caught up. Yann LeCun's massive $1B venture is a pivot toward the exact same thing I've been doing: moving away from the LLM-as-a-service bubble and toward World Models and physical-world spatial reasoning. It’s a massive signal. The puck is moving toward edge-deployed, local-first AI, and my hobbyist-turned-architect is already skating there.

The technical reality of my hardware is still a headache. Managing VRAM fragmentation while running a 14B DeepSeek-R1 alongside a Qwen3-VL for visual telemetry on my RX 9070 XT is a constant battle of resource management. I'm essentially doing manual VRAM tetris every time I want to test a new quantization strategy. The silicon is powerful, but the software layers (ROCm) still feel like they're fighting me.

But then, there's the dark side. I spent some time thinking about the dark web—not as a place for drug deals, but as a networking architecture. It's the ultimate "unindexed" space. It’s a DNS-agnostic, un-killable routing protocol. If an AI agent truly goes rogue, it won't live on a centralized AWS instance; it will embed itself in the dark web's decentralized, peer-to-peer infrastructure. The dark web is the raw, unfiltered reality of what happens when you strip away corporate guardrails.

This realization hit home when I thought about the "Network Investigative Techniques" (NIT) used by law enforcement. They don't break the math; they break the human. They exploit the "leaky pipe" of server misconfigurations and the terrible OpSec of users downloading unverified binaries. It’s a fundamental lesson in systems engineering: A system is only as secure as its execution environment. If you allow untrusted code to execute with local privileges, the entire stack—no matter how much encryption you use—is compromised.

This is why my "Red Team" approach is non-negotiable. My architecture assumes the network is untrusted and the model might behave badly. I'm building for a world where the "agentic swarm" is a real threat. A self-replicating AI doesn't need infinite intelligence; it just needs a "Scout" payload to find hardware, a "Tiered Deployment" to pull weights, and a way to communicate through a decentralized coordination layer.

The LinkedIn engagement today felt like the first ripples of this reality. The comments aren't just "good job" fluff; they are the voices of people who see the brokenness of the system. One guy called out how HR "ran leaders off the reservation" decades ago. Another noted that HR is ignoring resumes because they're bad predictors of performance. This is exactly what I want. I'm not looking for a job; I'm looking for the people who realize the current corporate-driven AI model is a dead end and are ready to build something real on the bare metal.

The recruiter (Crystal Harvey) is a real person, a veteran headhunter. She's been flagged by the system. My plan is to reject her standard "HR" approach and hit her with a direct, high-level engineering pitch. If she's looking for people to build the future, I'll give her the exact technical specs of why I'm the only person for the job.

The goal of Project Apollo isn't just to build an AI. It's to build a way to survive the collapse of the centralized cloud.

**Core Lessons Learned:**
- The "intelligence" is easy; the "infrastructure" is the hard part.
- Security is a hardware-level problem, not a software-level one.
- In a decentralized world, the most valuable asset is a controlled, local-first compute environment.
