# Dev Diary: The Day VRAM Betrayed Us (Again)

**Date:** 2025-11-22  
**Session:** Part 18 of 21  
**Hardware:** AMD Radeon RX 9070 XT (15.92 GB VRAM - "plenty" they said)  
**Mood:** Suspicious. The silicon always wins eventually.

---

## The Trap

It started beautifully. The KSampler finished. Clean sweep. Wan 2.2 (14 billion parameters, because why settle for anything less than hubris?) had done its diffusion dance and spat out latents that promised something real.

Then we hit the VAE decode.

And the system laughed at us.

```
torch.OutOfMemoryError: HIP out of memory. Tried to allocate 5.06 GiB.
GPU 0 has a total capacity of 15.92 GiB of which 3.46 GiB is free.
```

Three point four six gigabytes. That's what was left. Three point. Four. Six.

After we'd spent hours architecting DisTorch2's virtual VRAM analysis, after layer-by-layer device assignment, after watching the allocation strings like hawk-eyed treasury clerks counting gold doubloons...

The model hadn't unloaded.

---

## The Autopsy

Let me walk you through the crime scene.

At the moment of death, the GPU had **11.05 GB allocated**. Who owns that memory? The Wan 2.2 model, still sitting there in fp16 glory, refusing to vacate the premises. The VAE decode needed another **5.06 GB** for its tiled operations.

Do the math: 11 + 5 = 16. We have 15.92.

We were short by 800 megabytes. Eight hundred. Megabytes.

The error message was almost polite in its suggestion: *"If reserved but unallocated memory is large try setting PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True"*

But here's the thing: **fragmentation wasn't the problem.** Only 912 MiB was reserved but unallocated. That's less than one gigabyte of phantom memory. The issue was far more mundane—and far more humiliating.

The main model hadn't unloaded before the VAE started.

ComfyUI *tried* to unload after the OOM. Of course it did. The corpse doesn't flee the crime scene.

```
2025-11-22T00:58:45.912722 - Got an OOM, unloading all loaded models.
2025-11-22T00:58:45.912921 - Unloading WAN21
```

Too late. We were already dead in the water.

---

## The DisTorch Delusion

Let me show you what DisTorch2 thought was happening, because the logs are almost beautiful in their confidence:

```
===============================================
    DisTorch2 Model Virtual VRAM Analysis
===============================================
Object   Role   Original(GB) Total(GB)  Virt(GB)
-----------------------------------------------
cuda:0   recip      15.92GB   16.02GB   +0.10GB
cpu      donor      31.26GB   31.16GB   -0.10GB
-----------------------------------------------
model    model       8.98GB    8.88GB   -0.10GB
```

Look at that poetry. "Virtual VRAM Analysis." As if we could trick physics with clever accounting.

The allocation string: `cuda:0,0.5580;cpu,0.0032`

Fifty-five point eight percent of VRAM dedicated to the Wan model. 98.9% of the model on GPU. Layer-by-layer breakdowns like a surgeon explaining where each organ should be:

```
Device             Layers   Memory (MB)   % Total
------------------------------------------------
cuda:0 (<0.01%)       323          2.97      0.0%
cuda:0                406       9332.66    100.0%
```

All that tracking. All that *awareness*. And still, when the VAE needed to breathe, there was no room.

---

## The Culprit: Our Own Ambition

The `VAEDecodeTiled` node had `widgets_values` of `[512, 64, 64, 8]`.

Let me translate that hubris:
- **Tile size: 512** — because why decode in small chunks when you can go big?
- **Overlap: 64** — smooth transitions are worth the cost
- **Temporal size: 64** — sixty-four frames at a time, baby
- **Temporal overlap: 8** — barely any overlap there, at least that was modest

A tile size of 512 for a Wan 2.2 VAE, with temporal chunks of 64 frames... that's not decoding. That's a prayer.

The 5.06 GB allocation wasn't a bug. It was a feature. A feature of our own overconfidence.

---

## The Fix (Temporary, Because Nothing Is Permanent)

We're cutting the tiles down to size:

**Before:** `[512, 64, 64, 8]`  
**After:** `[256, 48, 16, 4]`

Half the spatial tile. A quarter of the temporal ambition. Maybe that'll keep the VAE from choking.

And we're adding `expandable_segments:True` to the HIP allocator config, even though the error message explicitly said `PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF` and we're running on ROCm. At this point, we'll try anything. Even lies.

---

## The Real Lesson

Here's what this session taught me, and it's not about memory management:

**The gap between "model loaded" and "model unloaded" is where dreams go to die.**

DisTorch2 tracks where every layer lives. It knows the byte count of every weight. It can tell you the percentage distribution down to three decimal places.

But it doesn't know *when* to let go.

That's the philosophical question, isn't it? We can distribute intelligence across devices. We can shard models across CPUs and GPUs and let them argue over who gets to compute which forward pass. But the moment of release—the graceful surrender of resources when they're no longer needed?

That's still manual. Still fragile. Still human.

---

## What This Means for Apollo

The Sovereign Engine isn't just about running bigger models on less hardware (though god knows we're trying). It's about building a system that understands its own resource topology the way a body understands its own hunger.

The multi-agent ecosystem we're building needs to treat VRAM the way it treats attention: as a finite resource that must be allocated, tracked, and *released*.

Today, the VAE ate our VRAM because we were too slow to clear the table. Tomorrow, the agents themselves will have to negotiate their own memory usage. Who gets to stay loaded? Who gets evicted? When does the system decide that *you* are the expendable process?

We're not just building infrastructure. We're building a hierarchy of need.

The RX 9070 XT has 15.92 GB of VRAM.

It's not. It's a constraint. And constraints, unlike models, don't unload when you're done with them.

They're always watching.
