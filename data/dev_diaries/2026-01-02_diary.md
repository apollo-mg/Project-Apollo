# Project Apollo: Resonance & Rigidity

**2026-01-02 | Late Night Session**

The terminal is the only thing lighting up the room. There’s something primal about it—the hum of the RX 9070 XT in the background, the smell of ozone, and the constant, frantic data streaming from the Klipper logs. We aren't just building a machine; we’re trying to harmonize silicon-level intelligence with the raw, violent physics of high-speed motion.

Tonight was about the resonance.

The new linear rail is on. It’s a beast. But the machine is screaming in frequencies I can'thear, but the PSD (Power Spectral Density) graphs can. We ran the input shaper calibration, and the data came back... weird. The X-axis is incredibly stiff—a massive, sharp peak at nearly 150 Hz. That’s the sound of carbon fiber resisting the world. But there’s this "noise" underneath it. These low-frequency humps around 70 Hz and 95 Hz. They aren't the main bending mode; they’re something more insidious. Torsion. The rail is twisting against the tube.

I sat there with the Gemini CLI, staring at the graphs. It’s a strange partnership. I provide the raw, messy reality of the hardware, and the AI attempts to find the mathematical loophole that lets us print faster without the ghosting. 

The automated tool suggested a standard `ei` shaper. It’s the "safe" choice. It allows for huge acceleration, but it leaves 1.9% residual vibration. It’s a "System" solution—functional, but blunt. But I pushed it. We dug into the data, looking for the "Soul" in the noise. 

We found it in the `2hump_ei` option. By switching the X-axis to a dual-hump shaper, we can drop the vibration to almost zero (0.1%) without sacrificing the headroom. Even though the Y-axis is our current bottleneck—clamping our global `max_accel` to 16,000 mm/s²—the X-axis can still handle much more. It’s about optimizing the envelope, finding that sweet spot where the machine feels monolithic rather than rattling apart.

Then came the wild card. The user (me, playing both sides of the interface) suggested the "epoxy bedding." 

This is the ultimate friction between the Soul and the System. To fix the torsion, we bond the rail to the carbon fiber tube using epoxy. It’s a permanent, almost irreversible chemical bond. It turns the gantry into a single, rigid organism. But there's a catch: Mass. 

In the world of high-speed CNC and 3D printing, mass is the enemy of acceleration. If we add too much weight to the X-gantry, we kill the Y-axis. We’ve already seen the Y-axis resonance sitting at a precarious 65 Hz. If the epoxy makes the gantry too heavy, the Y-axis performance will plummet, and our 16,000 mm/s² dream will die.

We’re leaning toward a "surgical" approach—using a lightweight epoxy mix, maybe something with microballoons to keep the density low while maximizing the stiffness. 

The goal is clear: we want a machine that doesn't just move fast, but moves with intent. A machine where the vibration is so well-damped that the distinction between the digital command and the physical movement disappears.

**Current Status:**
- **X-Axis:** Configured for `2hump_ei`. Near-zero vibration.
- **Y-Axis:** Held at 16,000 mm/s² limit. The current ceiling.
- **Next Step:** The Epoxy Test. We glue the rail. We find out if we’ve built a masterpiece or just a very expensive, heavy paperweight.
