# Dev Diary: 2026-01-27

## The Physics of a Lie

Today was a battle against the ghost in the machine. I’ve spent the last several hours trying to pin down a 76 Hz resonance that refused to go away. I thought I had a loose component—a bad bearing, a loose belt, a loose bolt. I cleaned the bearings, tightened the frame, re-calibrated everything. Nothing changed. 

The machine was lying to me.

I ran the resonance test on the Y-axis again after the maintenance. The graph was identical. A sharp, clean spike at 76 Hz. In a world of messy, jagged noise, a clean spike is a signal of structural truth. It wasn't matter how much I tightened the bolts; the vibration wasn't come from "slop," it came from the very architecture of the metal itself.

## The Ghost in the Frame

The real breakthrough came when I realized the scale of the problem. The machine isn't just vibrating; it’s *racking*. 

The Ender 6 frame is a tall, boxy rectangle. When the Y-axis slams that heavy Carbon Fiber X-gantry back and forth at 15,000 mm/s², the entire frame is being twisted like a piece of taffy. The frame is acting like a tuning fork.

I ran a diagnostic rotation to see if it was the workbench—the theory that the table was wobbling. It wasn't. The resonance stayed. It's the printer's soul being too heavy for its skeleton.

The input shaper is doing something incredible, though. Klipper’s algorithm found a way to "hack" the physics. It identified a harmonic at 149.8 Hz and used a complex `3HUMP_EI` filter to suppress the 76 Hz fundamental frequency. It’s a mathematical mask, but it's a working one. It allows me to maintain high acceleration, even if the metal is screaming underneath the surface.

## The Unmasked Truth

I took it a step further. I moved the ADXL sensor from the nozzle to the top plate of the printhead. This was the moment of clarity. 

The nozzle-mounted data was lying to me—it was "masking" the true vibration because the nozzle's own mass was dampening the reading. When I mounted it to the rigid aluminum plate, the truth came out. The 76 Hz spike vanished, replaced by a massive, brutal 60 Hz spike. 

The 60 Hz frequency is the "true" resonance of the frame racking. It's the fundamental frequency of the aluminum extrusion itself. The machine is physically limited by its own geometry.

## The Strategy: Hardware vs. Software

I've made the hard call. Instead of trying to fight the 60 Hz giant with more aggressive software filters (which would just round off the corners), I've decided to embrace a "Split Strategy."

I've pushed the `max_accel` back up to 15,000 mm/s² in the firmware. This allows the Infill and Travel moves to maintain their high-speed identity. However, I will now use the slicer to throttle the "visible" parts. I'll cap the Outer Walls at 8,000 mm/s² to keep them within the safe zone of the `MZV` shaper, while letting the rest of the machine run at its true mechanical limit.

It’s a compromise between the Soul (the beautiful, high-speed prints) and the System (the vibrating, 60 Hz metal frame). 

## Next Up: Pressure Advance

Now that the mechanical identity of the machine is settled, it's time to go back to the fight. I need to tune the Pressure Advance on the Goliath. I suspect the "Extrusion Rate Smoothing" in Orca was the enemy here—trying to smooth out the flow was essentially fighting the Klipper firmware's attempt to regulate pressure. 

The goal: Pure, high-speed flow with zero corner bulge. If the hardware can't handle the speed, I'll find that limit in the volumetric flow rate.

The machine is finally honest. Now we see if it can actually print.
