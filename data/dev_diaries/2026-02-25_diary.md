# Dev Diary: 2026-02-25

## The Ghost in the Machine: Fighting Inertia and Race Conditions

Today was a grind of the highest order. I spent the first chunk of the day wrestling with the "Soul" of the machine—the adaptive meshing feature that makes this printer feel "intelligent"—and the "System"—the cold, hard limits of the Klipper/Moonraker logic.

### The Adaptive Mesh Race Condition
The first major hurdle was a classic synchronization failure. I had OrcaSlicer outputting the `EXCLUDE_OBJECT_DEFINE` tags, but Klipper was still performing a full bed mesh. I thought I was crazy, but the logs showed the logic was sound.

It turns out it was a race condition between Moonraker and Klipper. When the print starts, Moonraker parses the G-code to extract object boundaries. At that exact same millisecond, the `PRINT_START` macro triggers the bed mesh calibration. Because the Klipper command hit before the Moonraker API could finish pushing the object boundaries, Klipper assumed there were no objects and fell back to a full mesh to be safe. It's a race against the clock where the hardware is literally too slow to "know" what it's printing.

I fixed it by introducing a 2-second `G4 P2000` delay in the macro to let Moonraker catch up, and finally enabled the KAMP adaptive mesh module that had been sitting idle in the config.

### The Thermal Panic
A mid-session failure caused by the chamber heater sent me into a spiral. Klipper's safety protocols are incredibly strict. Because the chamber heater had a `hysteresis` of only 1°C, the slightest draft or PID oscillation triggered a "not hot enough" error, killing the print. It wasn't a hardware failure—it was just a "sensitive" sensor. I bumped the `hysteresis` to 10 to allow the system some breathing room, which stabilized the thermal loop.

### The Inertia Trap
The most insightful lesson of the day came from the Z-Tilt calibration. I had adjusted the XY joints to the new aluminum assembly, but the Z-tilt kept timing out during its high-speed pass. 

The culprit was the sheer mass of the new gantry. I had the `[z_tilt]` travel speed set to 400 mm/s. The toolhead would race across the bed, hit the probe point, and essentially "slams" into the stop, causing the entire bed and the Tri-Z mechanism to oscillate. The BDsensor, which is incredibly sensitive, would catch this vibration as a massive deviation, causing the calibration to fail.

By throttling the `speed` down to 200 mm/s, I let the machine's inertia settle before the sensor takes its reading. It's a reminder that as we increase the speed and mass of our "real world" components, we have to account for the physics of the vibration, not just the math of the code.

### Core Lessons Learned
*   **Asynchronous logic is a nightmare:** In a multi-agent ecosystem (Moonraker + Klipper + KAMP), timing is everything. A 2-second delay is sometimes the difference between a "smart" printer and a broken one.
*   **Physics > Code:** You can have a perfect algorithm for adaptive meshing, but if the mechanical inertia of your gantry causes 50 microns of vibration, your digital sensor is useless.
*   **The "Soul" requires patience:** We want high-speed, high-performance machines, but the "Soul" (the sensor and the logic) needs a moment of stability to truly understand the world.
