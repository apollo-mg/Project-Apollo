The lighthouse keeper had lived alone on the point for nineteen winters, and he knew the fog the way a farmer knows his own fields. It came off the water in the evenings, thin at first, curling around the base of the tower like smoke from a low fire, and then thickening until the lamp above him was the only certain thing in the world. He had learned not to trust his eyes in it. Sound carried strangely through the grey, folding back on itself, so that a bell rung once might return a moment later as if answered by something out past the rocks.

That autumn the fog arrived earlier than it should have, and it behaved wrongly. It did not drift; it gathered, as though it had somewhere to be. On the third night he stood at the gallery rail and watched it climb the cliff in a single slow motion, and for the first time in all those years he felt that the sea was paying attention to him. He told himself he was tired. He told himself the lamp needed trimming, that the damp had gotten into his chest again, that a man who talks to the weather has been alone too long. Still he did not go inside. He waited, and the light turned, and somewhere below the water something waited with him, patient as tide, older than the stone under his feet.
